On Monday morning, Taiga woke to the sound of somebody looking for a shoe.

He could not work out who. The voice came from the courtyard, followed by another voice from somewhere upstairs and then Carmen saying something that included a number, a name and the Spanish word for late.

Beside him, Leo pulled the pillow over his head.

“School,” he explained from underneath it.

“I got that much.”

“Then you know everything you need to know.”

Taiga checked his phone. It was seven twelve.

He put it facedown again and considered going back to sleep, but the bed had become too warm, his shoulder was trapped beneath Leo, and whoever owned the shoe had apparently decided the whole property should participate in finding it.

“Your family had a party until one in the morning.”

Leo moved the pillow enough to expose one eye.

“The people looking for shoes went to bed before us.”

“Unfair advantage.”

“I’ll tell Antonio you object to his bedtime.”

From below, a door shut. Taiga caught Carmen’s voice again, closer now, followed by Manuel answering. A car started, stopped, then started properly.

Leo rolled away from him at last.

“Do you want coffee?”

“I want whoever finds the shoe to announce it.”

“That’s not how it works. You have to assume success.”

Taiga sat up and stretched his arm until feeling returned to it. Enzo’s shirt still hung over the chair, and the photo book lay closed beside the monitor. One of the birthday envelopes had slipped halfway underneath it.

He reached for his own shirt.

Leo caught the hem before he could pull it over his head and drew him down for a kiss.

Taiga braced a hand on the mattress.

“Coffee first would improve this.”

“Good morning to you too.”

Leo kissed him again anyway, then let him up.

By the time they went downstairs, the courtyard had emptied considerably. Carmen stood near the gate with her bag on her shoulder, explaining something to Dani, who was trying to put an earbud in without appearing to stop listening. Manuel waited outside. Antonio crossed behind them wearing both shoes, which Taiga found reassuring.

Ramón came down the exterior stairs carrying a rectangular parcel under one arm. He greeted Taiga, looked at Leo and launched into something involving the chairs still stacked beside the wall.

Leo answered while reaching into the kitchen for two mugs.

Taiga caught *mañana*. Tomorrow.

Then Ramón pointed at the stack.

“*Tomorrow?*” Taiga asked.

Ramón turned toward him with interest.

Leo handed Taiga a mug before his uncle could expand the conversation.

“We’re taking those back tomorrow. The borrowed ones.”

“Which are the borrowed ones?”

“The ugly ones.”

Taiga looked at the stack.

“That does not narrow it down.”

Ramón asked what he had said. Leo translated, and his uncle spent the next thirty seconds defending chairs that apparently did not belong to him.

Isa came out carrying her bag and said one sentence. Ramón picked up his parcel.

“*See you later,*” she told them.

Taiga answered in Spanish. She smiled and followed Ramón through the gate.

Carmen called something from outside, Leo called back, Dani finally put the earbud in, and then they were gone too.

The quiet arrived so suddenly that Taiga heard the refrigerator start.

He looked toward the gate.

Leo took a sip of coffee.

“There. Shoe crisis over.”

Taiga listened. A radio played from Mercedes’s kitchen, and traffic was still audible beyond the wall, but he could hear separate sounds now: a spoon against a cup, a bird somewhere above them, the soft scrape of Mercedes moving a chair.

“Does everybody leave?”

“Usually. Moma and Papá already should’ve left, actually. Tío too. Tía’s hours change.”

“And your abuela?”

“Is about to have a much better morning.”

“*What are you saying?*” Mercedes said.

Leo smiled toward the kitchen.

“*I said you love us.*”

Mercedes replied too quickly for Taiga to follow, though her expression supplied enough.

Breakfast was bread, fruit and eggs left covered on the counter. Leo found plates while Taiga took the mugs to the table. One birthday decoration hung crookedly above it, the end of its string caught on a nail.

Taiga pointed with his coffee.

“You keeping that until next year?”

“I’m considering it. Saves time.”

“It says twenty.”

“I could stay twenty.”

“You barely qualified the first time.”

Leo smiled around a mouthful of bread.

They ate without anyone adding food to their plates. Taiga had nearly finished before realizing it, and when he mentioned it, Leo offered to call his mother.

After breakfast they cleared what remained of the party. Leo held a bag open while Taiga took the decorations down; then they wiped the folding tables and carried one back to the storage space beneath the exterior stairs. Taiga had seen the door several times without knowing what was behind it.

A broom fell toward him when Leo opened it.

Taiga caught the handle.

“Good system.”

“Papá moved things to get the tables out.”

“Apparently he declared war on this one.”

They had to turn the table sideways to fit it past a stack of boxes. Leo directed from the far end, talking continuously about an entirely different occasion when Enzo had helped move furniture and wedged an armchair in a doorway.

“Why didn’t you measure it?” Taiga asked.

“We thought it would fit.”

“Based on what?”

“Looking at it.”

“That’s not a method.”

“It usually works.”

Taiga pushed his end a little farther in.

“Was Enzo trapped behind it?”

“For a while.”

“Then it worked.”

Leo laughed and nearly lost his grip.

When they came back out, Mercedes was reaching for one of the lighter chairs. Leo crossed over to take it, and she pointed toward the other side of the patio.

He moved it, then lifted the empty plant pot beside it.

“*This too?*”

Mercedes answered at greater length. Taiga understood *no* and *there*, which appeared to be the important parts. Leo put the pot down again and returned to the table they had been wiping.

“What was wrong with the pot?”

“Nothing. She wants it there.”

“Then why were you moving it?”

“I thought that was why she wanted the chair out of the way.”

Taiga handed him the cloth.

The remaining cake was in Carmen’s refrigerator. They found it after finishing, cut two slices and ate them standing in the kitchen because Leo said plates would create more washing-up.

Taiga looked at the fork in his hand.

“And these?”

“Small washing-up.”

“Your principles are very flexible.”

“They have to survive contact with cake.”

The controller worked once Leo found a cable.

Taiga stood behind the desk chair watching him untangle it from a knot that seemed to contain every electronic device he had owned since childhood.

“You could have done this on Friday.”

“You arrived on Friday. I had other things to do.”

“You pressed one button and gave up.”

“I’m sorry my repair service disappointed you during your welcome reception.”

Leo connected the controller, waited for a light, then held it up.

Taiga took it.

“Miracle.”

“You’re welcome. Sit down before I change my mind.”

They moved the chair aside and sat on the edge of the bed facing the monitor. Leo picked a racing game, partly because Taiga knew the controls and partly, Taiga suspected, because Leo had been playing it for years.

The suspicion became certainty at the first corner.

Leo’s car slid around it cleanly. Taiga hit a barrier, corrected too far and hit the opposite one.

“What the fuck is wrong with the steering?”

“You’re holding the accelerator.”

“It’s a racing game.”

“Yes, but sometimes a corner happens.”

Taiga leaned forward.

Leo reached past him to adjust the speaker volume, still driving with his other hand.

Taiga noticed.

“You’re doing that on purpose.”

“What, making it louder? I can barely hear it.”

“You know what I mean.”

Leo returned both hands to his controller, smiling.

He won the first race by an amount Taiga refused to discuss. The second was closer. By the third, Taiga had learned which turns required braking and which barriers could be used without losing too much speed.

“That is a terrible habit,” Leo said as Taiga bounced off a wall and came out ahead.

“Then overtake me.”

“I would if you’d stop driving across the entire road.”

“It’s available space.”

Leo made an offended noise and tried to pass on the inside. Taiga moved first. Both cars spun.

They spent the rest of the lap arguing about whose fault it was and finished behind a computer-controlled car neither had considered a threat.

Leo stared at the results screen.

“We lost to that thing.”

“You lost. I finished ahead of you.”

“You were sideways across the finish line.”

“Still crossed it.”

Leo started another race before Taiga could put the controller down.

At some point Mercedes appeared at the door. Taiga had not heard her come upstairs.

She looked at the monitor, then at Leo leaning forward with his elbows on his knees.

“*You’re losing?*”

Taiga understood that immediately.

“*Yes. He is.*”

Leo pointed at the corner of the screen.

“*That’s his car, Abuela.*”

Mercedes inspected it for another second, then shrugged and spoke too quickly for Taiga to follow.

She left before either of them could reply.

“What did she say?”

“That she thought I’d have learned by now.”

Taiga laughed.

“She’s right.”

“You’re in fourth.”

“Enjoy it while it lasts.”

Leo took the next turn badly because he was watching Taiga instead of the screen. Taiga passed him, whooped once before he could stop himself, and spent the remainder of the race defending a lead that ended three seconds before the finish.

He dropped backward onto the mattress.

Leo set his controller down with enormous care.

“I’m going to let you process that.”

“Do not fucking speak.”

“I respect your privacy during this difficult time.”

Taiga reached for a pillow. Leo caught it before it hit him, then leaned down and kissed the edge of his mouth.

“That was actually good. You almost had me.”

“I hate ‘almost.’”

“I’ve noticed.”

Leo settled beside him, one foot still on the floor. Through the open window came the radio and Mercedes speaking to somebody in the courtyard.

Taiga glanced at his phone. It was almost noon.

He had spent most of the morning cleaning up somebody else’s birthday and losing at a game he had thought he was good at. He was hungry again.

“Should we get lunch?”

Leo turned his head.

“Yeah. Then we can go out if you want.”

“Anywhere in particular?”

“I don’t know. We could walk somewhere, get something cold.”

Taiga considered it, then reached across Leo for the controller.

“After I beat you.”

Leo’s expression became delighted.

“Okay. But we should probably tell Moma you’re staying until August.”

By Tuesday, Taiga had discovered that learning a house involved considerably more than knowing where the bathroom was.

There were cloths for the kitchen and cloths for the floor. The drinking glasses in Carmen’s kitchen were not all supposed to migrate to Mercedes’s, even though everyone appeared to carry them there. One gate latch needed lifting before it would close. The shaded part of the patio moved far enough during the day to make a comfortable chair useless by lunch.

Leo knew these things without knowing he knew them.

“Not that one,” he said when Taiga reached for a cloth.

Taiga held it up.

“You just used this.”

“On the counter.”

“And I’m wiping a table.”

“That’s the counter one.”

Taiga looked between the cloth and the table.

“Explain the difference.”

Leo opened his mouth, stopped, then reached for another cloth.

“This one is for that.”

“Excellent. Very scientific.”

“You can take it up with Moma.”

Taiga accepted the other cloth.

He was less interested in challenging Carmen’s system than in learning enough of it to stop being corrected.

They took the borrowed chairs back that morning, three at a time between them, to a house a short walk away. The owner came to the door and thanked Leo, then looked at Taiga and asked a question he almost understood.

Something about staying.

Taiga glanced at Leo.

“She’s asking how long you’re here.”

“*A few weeks,*” Taiga said carefully.

The woman nodded and replied with several sentences in which he caught *Carmen* and *Sunday*. Leo answered. She disappeared inside and came back with a covered bowl.

They returned home carrying it.

“We brought furniture and acquired lunch,” Taiga said.

“Beans. They’re for Abuela.”

“How do you know?”

“She told me.”

“Right. Unfair advantage.”

After leaving the bowl with Mercedes, they went out again to buy bread and a few things Carmen had written down. Leo gave Taiga the list while he locked the gate.

Taiga recognized most of it.

“What’s this word?”

Leo leaned over his shoulder.

“She wants detergent.”

“I meant your mother’s handwriting.”

“That might also be detergent.”

Taiga shoved the list back at him.

The bakery was farther along than the place with the empanadas. Taiga recognized the turn before Leo pointed it out, though he would not yet have trusted himself to find it from another direction.

They passed a man unloading crates, and Leo stopped to greet him. Taiga caught a question about university, then the familiar sequence: football, a laugh, a gesture toward him.

He introduced himself before Leo could.

The man shook his hand and spoke slowly enough that Taiga understood.

“*You play too?*”

“*Yes. Same team.*”

“*What position?*”

Taiga knew the word because he had made Leo teach it to him before he arrived.

“Juego de delantero.”

The man nodded and launched into something much faster.

Taiga lasted perhaps five seconds.

Leo laughed at his expression and stepped back into the conversation. They talked while helping shift two crates away from the doorway, and when they finally moved on, Leo explained that the man supported a club Taiga disliked.

“That explains the opinions.”

“You didn’t understand his opinions.”

“I understood the shirt.”

They bought the bread, then detergent, then stopped outside a shop because someone inside called Leo’s name. This time it was a woman who had known Carmen for years. She asked after Dani, called her own daughter over to say hello and wanted to see a picture from the championship.

Leo found one on his phone.

Taiga stood beside him holding the bread and watched the woman enlarge the photograph until half of Leo’s face filled the screen.

“She’s going to find a pore,” he murmured.

Leo’s shoulders shook.

The woman looked up.

“*What?*”

“*He likes the picture,*” Leo said.

“Coward.”

Leo nudged him with an elbow.

At the next corner, Taiga took the bag of detergent without asking, freeing Leo’s hand. Leo caught his fingers with that hand instead.

They walked back like that until the narrow pavement forced them apart.

Near the gate, Leo asked whether he wanted to go to the beach.

Taiga looked at him.

“You mean now?”

“Not now. We have bread.”

“That has never stopped anyone.”

“I was thinking Friday. We can leave early, spend the day, come back before it gets too late. Boca Chica, probably.”

Taiga turned the idea over while Leo opened the gate.

“Are you actually going to tell me where we’re going this time?”

“I just did.”

“The birthday established a dangerous precedent.”

“That worked out.”

“It did,” Taiga admitted.

Leo smiled at him.

Taiga lifted the bag.

“Put this away. Then show me.”

They spent part of lunch looking at the coast on Leo’s phone. Taiga traced the route from where they were staying and realized that going to the beach involved traveling back across the city and farther east.

“Early,” he agreed.

Leo reached for another piece of bread.

“Worth it, though.”

Taiga looked at the photograph on the screen, then at him.

“Friday, then.”

Enzo’s directions to the bar arrived on Wednesday afternoon.

Taiga read them over Leo’s shoulder while they sat on the bedroom floor sorting laundry.

**Enzo:** same place

**Leo:** helpful

**Enzo:** the place with the table

**Leo:** every place has tables

**Enzo:** billiards you difficult man

Leo put the phone down.

“I know where he means.”

“Then why did you ask?”

“Because he deserves it.”

Taiga handed him a shirt.

“Who else is coming?”

“Luis, Mariela, Samuel. We all went to school together, but Luis lived closer to Enzo. Mariela used to sit behind me in math.”

“Should I be worried?”

“About math?”

“About what she knows.”

Leo considered that for longer than Taiga liked.

“She remembers things selectively.”

“So do you.”

“Exactly. We’ll be fine.”

Dani appeared at the open door carrying a folded towel. She tossed it onto the sofa, then looked at the clothes between them.

“*That’s mine.*”

Leo looked down at the dark shirt in his hand.

“*This is mine.*”

“*You gave it to me in December.*”

“*I let you wear it.*”

Dani said something much longer. Taiga caught *December* again and a word he thought meant suitcase.

Leo held the shirt out to her.

“What happened?” Taiga asked.

“She’s claiming abandonment.”

“It was in my drawer for five months,” Dani said in English. “He only wants it because he can see it.”

“I’m sitting right here.”

“I know. That’s why I explained.”

She took the shirt and left.

Taiga watched her go.

“You should label your property.”

“Wouldn’t help. She can read.”

They finished the laundry, hung the damp things and ate before going out. Carmen asked where they were going. Leo told her, and she reminded him of something Taiga did not catch.

Outside, Taiga asked.

“Mother’s Day on Sunday. Dani and I are getting something tomorrow.”

Taiga frowned.

“Mother’s Day was weeks ago.”

“Here it’s Sunday.”

“Your calendar really is different.”

Leo laughed and bumped his shoulder against Taiga’s.

“Wait until you find out we also have normal Tuesdays.”

They walked to the bar after sunset. It was close enough that Leo took them there without checking anything, past a stretch of shuttered storefronts and a corner where two men were talking beside a parked motorbike.

The bar’s front stood open to the street. A television hung above the counter, competing with music from a speaker farther inside. The pool table occupied most of the back room. One side had more space than the other, and the chairs along the wall looked positioned by people who had learned exactly where a cue could hit them.

Enzo was already leaning against the table.

“Finally.”

Leo checked his phone.

“We’re four minutes early.”

“I was emotionally ready before the agreed time.”

“That sounds like your problem.”

A woman sitting beside the wall laughed. She wore her hair tied back and had a canvas bag wedged under her chair. Before Leo could reach her, she stood and hugged him.

“*You got taller,*” she said.

“*No, I didn’t.*”

“*Then stand normally. You always do that in pictures.*”

She pressed a palm against his raised shoulder. Leo dropped it.

Taiga liked her immediately.

“Mariela,” Leo told him. “This is Taiga.”

“I know,” she said in English. “He sent pictures.”

Taiga looked at Leo.

“How many?”

“Reasonable amount.”

Mariela made a small movement with her hand that suggested a quantity Leo would dispute.

The man beside her got up next. Luis was broad through the shoulders, wore a plain gray T-shirt and shook Taiga’s hand before pointing toward the pool table.

“*You play?*”

“*A little,*” Taiga answered.

Luis nodded as if that was the only introduction they needed.

Samuel arrived from the counter with bottles held between his fingers. He set them down carefully, apologized in Spanish to nobody in particular and greeted Leo with an arm around his shoulders.

He had a soft voice that Taiga struggled to separate from the music.

Leo repeated his name and added, in English, “He’s the only one here who ever returned a borrowed pen.”

Samuel heard *pen* and pointed at Enzo.

“*He still has mine.*”

Enzo waved him away.

“Historical dispute.”

They sorted drinks and money, then Luis began racking the balls. Taiga chose a beer after checking what the others had ordered, and Leo got the same.

Mariela spoke English comfortably, though she sometimes stopped to ask Leo for a word. Luis knew enough to ask Taiga about football; Samuel mostly stayed in Spanish, speaking more slowly when he addressed him.

Among themselves, they accelerated past anything Taiga could follow.

He caught names. A teacher, perhaps. Someone’s brother. A repeated phrase that made everyone look at Leo.

Leo began objecting before Taiga understood enough to ask.

“What did you do?”

“I improved something.”

Mariela turned toward Taiga.

“He made us change our whole presentation.”

Leo spread his hands.

“It was bad.”

“It was finished.”

“Those can both be true.”

She looked back at Taiga.

“We were supposed to talk about our plans after school. Just stand there and talk. He wanted slides, pictures, music—”

“One song. At the beginning.”

“—and a video.”

“Because Samuel had the camera.”

Samuel heard his name and contributed a quiet sentence.

Leo looked wounded.

“You said you liked the video.”

Samuel repeated himself more slowly for Taiga.

“*I liked going home.*”

Taiga laughed. He had understood almost all of it.

Leo pointed at him.

“You’re not allowed to join this. You weren’t there.”

“You brought me.”

Enzo leaned across the table.

“He put credits at the end.”

Taiga turned slowly toward Leo.

“For a school presentation?”

“People contributed.”

“Did the pen get a credit?”

Samuel laughed so hard he had to put his bottle down.

Luis tapped the cue against his shoe and said something. Leo answered, then turned toward Taiga.

“He says we’re playing before somebody makes another presentation.”

That seemed reasonable.

Luis and Mariela took the first game against Leo and Taiga. Enzo objected to being left out until Samuel reminded him that he had played two games already.

Leo handed Taiga a cue.

“We should establish something before we start.”

“You’re about to tell me you’re good at this.”

“No. I’m about to tell you Luis thinks he’s good at this, and if we lose, he’ll become unbearable.”

Luis looked over.

“*I am good.*”

“He understood you,” Taiga said.

“He knows when he’s being discussed.”

Taiga tested the cue in his hand. He had played before, but not recently enough to like having an audience.

Leo saw him considering the table.

“You want me to break?”

“Go ahead.”

Leo’s break scattered the balls without pocketing anything. Luis took his time over the next shot, sank a solid and left himself an awkward angle.

Mariela told him something.

He shook his head and bent over the table.

The ball stopped at the lip of the pocket.

She looked at Taiga.

“He never listens to me.”

“Is it contagious here?”

“It seems to be.”

Leo leaned against his shoulder while Luis stepped away from the table.

“We’re stripes. You’ve got the yellow one.”

“I can see it.”

“Just checking.”

Taiga lined up the shot. The angle looked manageable until Leo added, softly, “Don’t hit it too hard.”

Taiga straightened.

“Would you like to play both turns?”

Leo put his hands up.

“I’ll be over here supporting quietly.”

“You have never done that.”

Taiga sank the stripe, then missed the next one by enough that Enzo inhaled audibly.

Taiga pointed the cue at him.

“Choose your words carefully.”

“I was breathing.”

“Try less.”

The game moved slowly because everyone talked through it. Mariela asked Taiga about Olympus, then about New York. Luis wanted to know whether he and Leo trained every day. Taiga answered what he could in Spanish and looked to Leo when the question became too complicated.

Once, he started answering a question about how long he had played football with how many weeks he planned to stay. Leo caught it after *three* and started laughing.

“What did I say?”

“Wrong question. He’s asking how long you’ve been playing.”

Taiga shut his eyes briefly.

Luis repeated the sentence, separating the words this time.

Taiga heard the difference.

“Fine. Again.”

Nobody minded. Luis listened to the answer, nodded, and then became far more interested in the shot Mariela was about to attempt.

Taiga appreciated that.

He could get a sentence wrong without the entire evening becoming a Spanish lesson.

When their team won, it was because Leo pocketed the last stripe with a shot he appeared to have intended, followed by Taiga managing the eight after spending so long calculating the angle that Samuel offered him a chair.

Leo caught him around the shoulders.

“There we go!”

“You almost made me hit it.”

“You already hit it.”

“I’m preparing for next time.”

Luis inspected the table, then pointed at one pocket and began explaining something to Mariela.

She listened without expression.

“Excuses,” she told Taiga.

“I got that.”

The next game belonged to Enzo and Samuel. Taiga and Leo sat together along the wall, knees pressed against each other because the chair arrangement left little alternative.

Enzo missed an easy shot while still explaining why it was easy.

Leo slid sideways until his mouth was close to Taiga’s ear.

“He’s been doing that since we were fifteen.”

Taiga watched Enzo examine the cue tip.

“What, losing?”

“Explaining what should have happened.”

Mariela heard him and turned.

“*You explain too.*”

“*Better,*” Leo said.

She answered too fast for Taiga, and Leo laughed instead of translating. Taiga let the sentence go.

Later, Luis pulled out his phone to show Leo something. Taiga saw a photograph of a half-built cabinet before the screen moved away. Mariela was telling him about the bus she had missed that morning, switching into Spanish for a phrase and then trying to find the English.

Taiga recognized one word.

“Wait. You were waiting at the wrong place?”

“Yes! That’s what I’m saying. They moved—”

She stopped, asked Leo for a word, and Leo answered immediately before looking back at Luis’s phone.

“Stop. Bus stop,” she continued. “They moved it.”

Taiga nodded and she finished the story, including the part where she had been too annoyed to ask anyone why nobody else was waiting.

Across from them, Samuel quietly pocketed another ball while Enzo spoke.

They stayed for a second drink and another round. Taiga won one game, lost the next and learned that Luis’s seriousness at the table lasted only until Mariela leaned into his field of vision to eat a chip.

At some point the group began arguing about a beach trip from years earlier. Taiga understood almost nothing except Leo’s name and a number that kept changing.

Leo eventually supplied the explanation.

“They’re saying I lost a sandal.”

“Did you?”

“The water took it.”

Mariela leaned around him.

“He tried to retrieve it with the other sandal.”

Taiga stared.

Leo took a long drink.

“I was fourteen.”

“And then?”

Leo set the bottle down.

“Then I needed shoes.”

Taiga laughed hard enough that Leo threatened to cancel Friday.

They left together a little before eleven. Mariela made Leo send her Taiga’s number so she could send the photograph she had taken of them arguing over the pool table. Samuel offered a hand again; Luis told Taiga, carefully, that they would play next time.

Taiga understood him.

“*Next time I win again.*”

Luis smiled and shook his head.

Enzo walked with them to the corner before turning off in the other direction.

“Good work tonight,” he told Taiga.

“At what?”

“Surviving the witnesses.”

“I learned about the sandals. Worth it.”

Leo pushed Enzo toward his street.

“Go home.”

Enzo laughed and left them.

On the walk back, Leo reached for Taiga’s hand.

“Did you have a good time?”

Taiga looked at him.

“They’re good. I like them.”

“I thought you would.”

“Luis takes pool too seriously.”

“He does.”

“Mariela hates your presentations.”

“She loved the presentation.”

“And Samuel’s funnier than Enzo.”

Leo laughed.

“Please tell Enzo that.”

Taiga pulled his phone out when it buzzed.

Mariela had sent the picture. Leo stood beside him with one hand raised, apparently mid-explanation; Taiga was holding the cue and looking at him with open disbelief.

He showed Leo.

“That’s your face when you’re wrong.”

“That’s your face when you’re refusing advice.”

Taiga saved it anyway.

On Thursday afternoon, Carmen asked for something while Taiga was setting glasses on the patio table.

He caught *drawer* and *blue*, but the word between them disappeared. Leo was beside him cutting open a bag of bread.

Taiga glanced toward the kitchen.

“*Which drawer?*”

Carmen pointed.

“*The small one. Beside the stove.*”

That he understood. He went inside, opened it and found scissors with blue handles.

When he brought them back, Carmen thanked him and continued opening the package in front of her.

Leo slid a piece of bread onto Taiga’s plate.

“The blue ones,” he said in English.

“I worked it out.”

“I know. I was giving you the word.”

He said *tijeras*, slowly, then at the speed Carmen had used.

Taiga tried it. Leo corrected the middle sound.

From the other side of the table, Dani repeated it too, then added in English, “He says it like he’s going to use them on someone.”

“I might.”

She considered that.

“Then it’s fine.”

Leo laughed and nearly dropped the bread.

The afternoon filled around them. Antonio came home with something folded inside a school notebook and refused to show anybody until Sunday. Isa arrived later, put her bag away and asked Taiga to pass a glass. He did it without checking with Leo.

He still lost the thread as soon as Carmen and Ramón started talking across each other. This time he finished his food while they argued, then asked Leo about one word that had occurred three times.

Leo had to think about the context before explaining it.

“Wait. Which part were you listening to?”

“The part where your uncle got louder.”

“That was most of it.”

Afterward, Leo went with Dani to pick up what they had arranged for their mother. Taiga went along. He had no separate plan, and Leo had asked him while handing him his shoes.

Dani spent the walk telling them exactly why she had rejected Leo’s first suggestion. Taiga understood enough to establish that it had been another mug.

“You bought her a mug last year,” Dani said in English when he asked.

“It was a good mug.”

“She still has it.”

“That’s not an argument against another one.”

“She has a cupboard.”

Taiga sided with Dani.

They returned with a small wrapped package Leo would not let him inspect, something for Mercedes and Isa, and a bag of fruit Dani had bought because she wanted it immediately.

That evening, while Manuel and Ramón watched television inside and Carmen spoke to Isa in the patio, Taiga found Leo sitting on the lower step near the closed gate.

Leo shifted his feet to make room.

Taiga sat beside him and peeled one of Dani’s purchases.

“Five minutes?”

“Something like that.”

Taiga separated the fruit and offered him half.

Leo took it.

They could still hear the television from there, though the dialogue no longer demanded attention. A motorbike passed outside. Leo leaned back on his hands and looked up at the strip of sky above the wall.

Taiga ate, pulled a piece of white pith from his thumb and watched an ant investigate the step below them.

After a while Leo pointed at the peel.

“You’re getting good at those.”

“It’s an orange, Leo.”

“You made a mess yesterday.”

“I was using a knife.”

“And today you’ve evolved.”

Taiga flicked a small piece of peel at his knee.

Leo laughed, picked it up and added it to the rest.

When they went inside again, he asked Manuel where the spare beach towels were. Taiga followed him upstairs to find them.

---

They left early on Friday, carrying towels, water, sunscreen and enough food to make Carmen less suspicious of the enterprise.

Taiga had packed the bag the night before. Leo unpacked half of it in the morning to find the sunscreen, which was in the outside pocket.

“You watched me put it there.”

“I was looking at you.”

“That is not going to work.”

“It worked a little.”

Taiga took the bottle from him and put it back.

They crossed the patio while Mercedes was drinking coffee. She asked where they were going, listened to Leo’s answer and told them something that made him nod.

“What?”

“Bring the towels back.”

“That’s her concern?”

“She knows me.”

Taiga thought of the sandals.

“Fair.”

Getting to Boca Chica occupied much more of the morning than Leo’s description of a beach day had suggested. They had to travel into the city first, then find the onward bus east. Taiga followed him through the changes, trying to attach landmarks to the route and losing them whenever they turned onto another road.

On the last bus they found seats together. Leo gave him the window without asking, then reached across him to adjust the curtain so the sun stopped falling directly on his face.

Taiga moved the bag onto his lap.

“How many times have you done this?”

“To Boca Chica? Lots. Not always this way.”

“With your family?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes friends. Papá used to want everybody ready ridiculously early, and then somebody would forget something and we’d leave at exactly the same time anyway.”

“Sounds familiar.”

“Moma always brought food. Enough food that we could’ve stayed there.”

“You inherited that.”

Leo looked at the bag.

“That’s hers.”

“You accepted it.”

“I like eating.”

Taiga passed him some water.

The city stretched longer than he expected. Traffic thickened, loosened, stopped again. Leo pointed out a junction they had passed on the drive from the airport, and Taiga recognized it a second too late, after a truck had blocked most of the view.

For the rest of the ride, they shared Leo’s earphones. He kept skipping songs until Taiga took the phone from him.

“Let one finish.”

“I’m finding a good one.”

“You’ve rejected the first eight seconds of six songs.”

“That’s enough for some songs.”

Taiga picked one and put the phone facedown.

Leo listened for a moment, then nodded reluctantly.

“Okay. That one can stay.”

By the time they got off, Taiga wanted to stretch his legs and find shade. Leo wanted him to look at the water.

They walked past places offering food and drinks, with sand appearing in patches along the edge of the pavement before the beach opened in front of them. The water was pale near the shore, deepening farther out. People were already in it, though there was still room along the sand to choose where to sit.

Leo stopped beside him.

Taiga took his sunglasses off for a better look.

“Okay,” he said.

Leo waited.

Taiga looked at him.

“You’re going to need a better review?”

“I wasn’t going to ask.”

“You absolutely were.”

Leo smiled.

Taiga looked back at the water, then started taking off his shoes.

“Good choice.”

That appeared to satisfy him.

They found a place with some shade, checked what two seats would cost and paid before putting their things down. Leo took off his shirt and dropped it over the bag. Taiga watched him for a moment before remembering the sunscreen.

“Turn around.”

Leo glanced at the bottle.

“You’re very direct on vacation.”

“Your shoulders are going to burn.”

He turned, still smiling.

Taiga spread sunscreen across his upper back while Leo read something on his phone. There were pale marks along one shoulder that Taiga had never noticed clearly before, and he passed his thumb over one.

“From what?”

Leo looked back.

“What are you looking at?”

“That mark on your shoulder.”

“Oh. Fell off a wall when I was a kid.”

“Why were you on a wall?”

“Because Enzo said I couldn’t get over it.”

Taiga considered the number of stories Enzo apparently appeared in.

“Your parents must love him.”

“They feed him. That’s close enough.”

When Leo returned the favor, he put a cold palm between Taiga’s shoulder blades without warning.

Taiga swore.

“Sorry. I thought you were prepared.”

“For being attacked?”

“For sunscreen.”

Leo worked it over his back, then bent and kissed his shoulder before handing the bottle back.

They waited in the shade, drinking water and watching two children attempt to bury a plastic bucket upright. The project seemed to have no practical purpose, but both children were deeply committed.

Taiga pointed.

“That one’s sabotaging it.”

“He’s helping.”

“He keeps taking sand out.”

“Maybe that’s his job.”

“What kind of job is that?”

Leo leaned back.

“You should go manage them.”

Taiga nudged his ankle with one foot.

They went into the water together, leaving their bag tucked beneath their seats. Taiga kept glancing back until Leo pointed out that they had barely gone anywhere yet.

“I can still see it.”

“So can I.”

“Then look at something else.”

Taiga looked at him.

Leo’s expression changed immediately.

“Not like that.”

Taiga splashed him.

Leo closed his eyes as the water hit his chest and chin, then pushed his hair out of his face.

“You have about three seconds.”

Taiga was already moving.

Running through water turned out to be a stupid choice. Leo caught his wrist before he got far, and their attempt to drag each other off balance ended with both of them crouched in the shallows, laughing and arguing over who had started it.

“You said look at something else,” Taiga told him.

“That wasn’t an invitation to violence.”

“You were vague.”

Leo scooped water toward him. Taiga blocked most of it and received the rest directly in his mouth.

He spat, wiped his face and looked at Leo.

Leo took a cautious step backward.

“Okay, that one was unfortunate.”

Taiga caught the waistband at Leo’s hip and pulled him back.

They settled eventually, after attracting one bored glance from a man standing nearby. Farther along the shore, somebody was calling children toward a table. A boat moved in the distance.

Leo leaned back into the water and stretched out his arms, floating for a few seconds before one foot touched bottom again.

Taiga stayed beside him.

“You used to come here a lot?”

“Enough. There are other beaches I like too, but this was easier when we were younger.”

“Was this where you lost the shoes?”

Leo sighed.

“One sandal initially.”

“And then?”

“You heard the testimony.”

“I want your version.”

“My version is that nobody helped.”

Taiga laughed.

Leo stood properly and pushed wet hair away from his forehead.

“I thought I could hook the strap with the other one. It made sense for approximately half a second.”

“And you’ve been paying for it ever since.”

“Exactly. Thank you for understanding.”

Taiga put a hand against his wet cheek and kissed him.

Leo smiled before their mouths separated.

“Was that sympathy?”

“A little.”

“I’ll take it.”

They stayed in the water until Taiga’s fingers started wrinkling, then went back to their things and ate most of Carmen’s food. Leo had packed a dry shirt badly enough that it was already creased. Taiga spread it over the back of the seat to make room in the bag.

His own phone was warm when he checked it. Caleb had sent a photograph of something on a computer screen and a message Taiga would need several minutes to understand.

Taiga sent back a photograph of the water.

**Caleb:** That does not answer my question.

Taiga smiled and put the phone away.

“Who’s that?” Leo asked.

“Caleb. He wants me to look at something.”

“Tell him I said hi.”

“He asked about something else.”

“Then answer him too.”

Taiga did, briefly, while Leo bought another cold drink. Afterward they walked along the sand, shoes in one hand, discussing whether to buy lunch despite having already eaten.

Leo wanted fish. Taiga wanted somewhere to sit out of the direct sun.

They found both.

The food took long enough that Leo began explaining a game he thought Taiga would like. Taiga listened, asked one question about how the scoring worked and watched Leo reach for his phone to find a video.

“You’re doing the presentation thing.”

Leo paused.

Then he put the phone away.

“I could describe it badly if that would make you happier.”

“Go on.”

“You run around. Stuff happens. Sometimes you die.”

“Excellent. I’m sold.”

Leo laughed, then explained properly anyway.

They took another swim later, closer to their seats this time, and stayed in the shade afterward until leaving began to sound less annoying than getting more sand into everything they owned.

Taiga packed while Leo shook out the towels. Leo managed to send sand directly onto his own shoes.

Taiga waited for him to notice.

“Not a word,” Leo said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were enjoying it.”

“I was enjoying it silently.”

They were both hungry again by the time they reached home.

Carmen asked if they had eaten. Taiga answered before Leo could.

“*Yes. Your food. And fish.*”

She looked at the bag, then at the two of them, and asked another question.

Taiga caught *tomorrow* instead of *again* and frowned.

Leo repeated the last word.

“*Hungry?*” Taiga asked.

Carmen nodded.

“*Yes. Again.*”

She laughed and waved them toward the kitchen.

Upstairs later, Leo tipped one shoe over the wastebasket. Sand poured out.

Taiga sat on the bed with the photo from the bar and a new one from the beach open on his phone. In the second, Leo had been trying to push his hair back when Taiga took it. His eyes were narrowed against the brightness, his other hand stretched toward the camera.

“Send me that,” Leo said.

“You haven’t seen it.”

“I saw you taking it.”

Taiga turned the phone.

Leo studied it.

“Oh, that’s terrible.”

“You just asked for it.”

“Send it anyway.”

Taiga did.

Leo set his shoes aside and climbed onto the bed beside him, warm from the shower. He looked at the screen again, then took the phone and swiped back to Mariela’s photograph.

“She got your expression exactly.”

“She got yours too.”

Leo zoomed in on his own raised hand.

“I was explaining the shot.”

“I know. You explained it for three minutes.”

“You made it.”

Taiga turned to look at him.

Leo held the stare, then started smiling.

Taiga took his phone back, put it on the desk and pulled Leo down beside him.

---

On Saturday, Taiga woke knowing where the coffee was.

He still had to ask Leo how much to put in, because the container had no useful instructions and Leo’s answer of *the usual amount* was not an amount. Once that was settled, they made breakfast while Manuel worked on something at the other end of the counter.

When Antonio came looking for Leo, he found both of them eating.

The kid put a notebook on the table and began explaining a drawing. Taiga recognized a goal, several people and what appeared to be a ball considerably larger than anyone’s head.

Leo asked questions, turned the notebook and took Antonio’s pencil to demonstrate something. Antonio took it back immediately and corrected him.

Taiga watched them argue over the drawing while he finished his coffee.

“What is that?” he asked, pointing at a shape near the edge.

“*You,*” Antonio said.

Taiga looked again.

“*Why?*”

Antonio pointed at the red hair, then said something else.

Leo leaned over.

“He says you’re complaining about the referee.”

Taiga considered the figure’s furious eyebrows.

“That looks about right.”

Leo translated, and Antonio looked pleased.

They spent the morning at home. The towels from the beach needed washing, Leo had promised Dani he would look at why her laptop kept filling its storage, and Taiga wanted another attempt at the racing game now that he knew the tracks.

Dani brought the laptop upstairs while Taiga was hanging the second towel. By the time he returned, Leo had found three copies of the same folder.

“*They’re different*,” Dani said.

“*They have the same name*.”

“*One says final*.”

“*All of them say final*.”

She leaned over his shoulder.

“*That one’s the actual final*.”

Taiga sat beside Leo and pointed at a fourth folder.

“What’s that?”

Dani followed his finger.

For several seconds she said nothing.

Leo turned toward her slowly.

She took the laptop.

“*Okay. I can do it*.”

After she left, Leo laughed into Taiga’s shoulder.

They played until lunch. Taiga won twice, lost more than twice and refused Leo’s suggestion that they calculate percentages.

Later they walked out together to buy a small bag of sweets for Sunday. Taiga asked for them himself, with Leo beside him, and understood the price on the second attempt.

On the way home, he recognized the bakery turn, the shop where they had stopped on Tuesday and the wall beside the street leading to the compound. He still would have needed directions from the bar if Leo had taken a different route back. Here, he knew which way to go.

Leo held out a hand for the bag.

Taiga moved it away.

“They’re for tomorrow.”

“I’m checking them.”

“You’re eating them.”

“I can do both.”

Taiga opened the bag, gave him one and took one for himself.

---

Sunday’s lunch was for Carmen, Mercedes and Isa.

Taiga had been warned by then, though being warned did not prevent Ramón from arriving downstairs with flowers large enough to block his own view of the step. Isa caught his elbow before he walked into a chair.

Leo and Dani gave Carmen their package together. Taiga watched her lift a pair of small silver earrings from the box. She held one against her ear for Dani to see, then pulled both her children down to kiss them. Dani had been right about the mug.

Mercedes opened hers more slowly, carefully saving the paper until Antonio lost patience and tried to help. She moved it out of reach without looking at him.

By lunchtime, there were flowers in two kitchens and a bag of sweets on the table. Manuel was handling the food, with Ramón offering advice from a distance that allowed him to avoid most of the work.

Taiga and Leo carried plates out together.

Carmen started to rise from her chair.

Manuel said something firmly enough that she sat down again, still giving instructions.

Taiga understood where the larger bowl was meant to go. He put it there, moved the glasses farther from Antonio’s elbow and went back for the serving spoons.

Leo was at the counter.

“Which ones?”

Taiga opened the drawer.

“These, isn’t it?”

Leo looked down at the spoons in Taiga’s hand.

“Yeah, those are the ones.”

They brought the rest outside.

Lunch lasted longer than Taiga expected because Mercedes had a story to tell and Ramón kept supplying corrections she refused to accept. Leo translated a little of it for him, then stopped when Manuel asked him something.

Taiga kept listening. He caught a place name he had heard on Tuesday, Carmen’s name, something about a dress. The story turned somewhere he could not follow, but by then Isa was laughing and Carmen was arguing with her mother, so he had a reasonable idea of who had done something embarrassing.

Dani supplied the missing piece in English.

“She borrowed it without asking.”

Taiga looked at Carmen.

Carmen pointed at Mercedes and replied too quickly for him.

Leo leaned closer.

“She says Abuela never wore it anyway.”

“That sounds familiar.”

Dani looked at the shirt Leo was wearing and smiled.

After eating, Taiga stayed at the table while Leo sat on the low step with Antonio, examining the paper gift he had kept hidden all week. Isa joined them. Antonio opened it again for her even though she had already seen it twice.

Mercedes asked Taiga about the beach.

He understood the question.

“*Very good. We go Friday.*”

“*You went,*” she corrected gently.

He repeated it, and she nodded.

“*With Julio?*”

Taiga looked across at Leo.

“*No. Other boyfriend.*”

Mercedes laughed so suddenly that Carmen turned.

Taiga kept his face straight.

Across the patio, Leo looked up from the paper in Antonio’s hands.

“What did you say?”

“Practicing my Spanish.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

Mercedes repeated the exchange. Leo stared at Taiga, then at her, and shook his head.

“I leave you alone for five minutes.”

“Your mistake.”

Leo pointed at him, but Antonio pulled his attention back before he could respond. Mercedes reached for her coffee.

Taiga stayed where he was until his own cup was empty.

Then he carried it inside, rinsed it and put it where Carmen kept the others. Someone had left the cloth beside the sink. He wiped the small pool of water around the tap, hung the cloth back over its hook and returned to the patio.

Leo was standing now, handing Antonio’s gift to Isa.

“Want to go upstairs?”

Taiga nodded.

“Your turn to lose.”

“That’s a very ambitious description of what’s about to happen.”

On the way through the kitchen, Taiga took two glasses from the cupboard. Leo filled them while he went ahead to move the chair away from the monitor.

By the time Leo came into the room, the game was loading.

Taiga held out the second controller.

“I picked the track.”

Leo set the glasses on the desk and sat beside him.

“I can see that.”

“Any complaints?”

“Plenty. Start it.”

Taiga did.
