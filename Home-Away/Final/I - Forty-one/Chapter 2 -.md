### Chapter 2 - 

Monday morning, Taiga had discovered that Caleb Morrison owned six alarms. Not six alarms set on one phone. Six separate alarms. One on his phone. One on his watch. One on the little digital clock beside his bed. One on his laptop. One on some kind of smart speaker that Taiga hadn't realized was connected to anything. And, inexplicably, one physical alarm clock that looked like it had survived the Cold War.

At 6:30, all of them went off within approximately twelve seconds of one another. Taiga sat upright.

“What the fuck?”

Caleb's hand emerged from beneath his blanket and slapped vaguely at the nightstand. The physical alarm continued shrieking. His phone joined it. Then his watch. Then a pleasant female voice announced from the speaker:

“Good morning, Caleb. It is six-thirty A.M.”

Taiga grabbed his pillow and threw it across the room. It hit Caleb in the head.

“Ow.”

“Make it stop.”

“I am.”

“You're not.”

Caleb sat up, hair pointing in four directions. Taiga stared at him.

“This is psychotic.”

“It works.”

“It clearly doesn't.”

"I'm awake."

Caleb shut them down one by one, completely unbothered. He found his phone under his pillow and silenced it. The room finally went quiet. Taiga lay back down. Closed his eyes. Caleb said, 

“I have an eight o'clock.”

“I don't care.”

“You have an eight-thirty.”

Taiga opened one eye.

“How do you know?”

“Your timetable is on the wall.”

Taiga looked toward the printed schedule taped above his desk. 
Caleb swung his legs out of bed.

“You should get up.”

Taiga pulled the blanket over his face.

“Die.”

There was silence. Then Caleb said, completely seriously, 

“That would probably make you late.” 

Taiga slowly lowered the blanket. Caleb was already walking toward the bathroom. Taiga stared after him.

---
After the orientation week, Olympus stopped pretending to be nice.

During orientation week, the university had been covered in smiling volunteers, directional signs, free drinks and people asking if anyone needed help.
Now it was Monday.
The signs were disappearing. The volunteers had gone back to their own classes. Nobody stood outside the humanities building handing out bottled water. 
Taiga approved. At least the place was being honest now.

His first real Political Institutions lecture almost ruined his mood. Taiga had chosen the course because he needed a general-education credit and its description sounded less terrible than Philosophy of Art.
Within twelve minutes, he reconsidered every decision that had led him there. 

Professor Hall spoke quickly. Not impossibly. Just quickly enough that Taiga realized his old habit of listening and trusting himself to remember everything was not going to work. 
He opened his laptop and started notes.
Three slides later he was behind.
Fine. He typed faster.

A student in the front row raised his hand and asked a question involving a political theorist Taiga had never heard of. Hall smiled.

“Excellent connection.”

Taiga stopped typing. The student elaborated. Several people nodded. Taiga stared at the back of his head. 
Who the fuck was that? Did people study before the first proper lecture? Was that a thing? 
Hall continued. Taiga's fingers returned to the keyboard. His notes suddenly looked inadequate. 
He opened the course portal. There was a reading list, and it was long. Very long. His stomach tightened.

Fine. He'll read tonight. 

Then Hall mentioned their first response paper. Eight hundred words.

Still fine. Then Hall said:

“And just to be clear, these are not summaries. If I want to know what the author said, I can read the author. I want you to engage with the argument. Challenge it, defend it, identify a problem, but show me that you understand what you're responding to.”

Taiga looked at the assigned reading. Then at Hall. Then back down. Challenge what argument? He had not even properly identified it yet. A small pulse of irritation climbed his neck. 
First class and he was already behind.

He forced himself to type again. The cursor blinked on an unfinished line while scholarship letter returned to his mind with unpleasant clarity.

**The Olympus Academic Merit and Leadership Award recognizes students demonstrating exceptional academic promise, resilience, community contribution and leadership potential—**

Academic promise. Right. Sure. He had probably been the diversity pick for assholes. Congratulations, we needed one emotionally constipated redhead.
Taiga almost laughed. The girl beside him glanced over. He covered it with a cough.

He close his eyes and took a deep breath.

Okay.
Enough spiraling.
Facts: first lecture, one student knew someone he didn't. That was because he had ignored the readings. That was on him and it was fixable.

He opened a fresh line at the top of the document.

**READ THE FUCKING CHAPTERS TONIGHT**

Then underneath:

**STOP BEING DRAMATIC**

He stared at the second line for a second before deleting it. Some things did not need written evidence.

Next, he had Calculus I at eleven-sharp and General Chemistry before lunch.

Taiga liked them slightly better. These were topics that he already covered in high school, so he wasn't starting from zero.

Still, his intuition that the usual method of listening and remembering wouldn't have helped here, proved right once again.

[insert something else here]

Callback began that afternoon. Taiga arrived earlier enough to watch an assistant set out mannequins and flat markers along the left side of the pitch.
At 16:00, Mercer called them in. Taiga looked around.
At the first phase there were at least 30-40 people. Now he counted around 15.

After his assistants completed the warming up session with the candidates, Mercer approached them. Clipboard in one hand, whistle in the other.

"Some of the guys you saw yesterday were better players than some of you. I corrected the same mistake twice with them. I corrected yours once and watched you spend the rest of the session trying to solve it."

He looked at all of them straight into their eyes.

"I don't need good players. I already have enough. I need people who can grow in my team and become better."

He then looked at his clipboard.

"Today we will see how do you actually fare on the field. I don't care how good you are at shooting, dribbling or whatever. I want to see if you can read the field and move accordingly."

Then he began calling them one by one and assigning a position. Most of them already played and already had a preferred one. 
Taiga, of course, had no idea.

"Akatora."

He approached.

"I'm assuming you've never played organized soccer."

Taiga's stomach clenched. Was it that obvious?

"No."

"You're pretty fast and stubborn enough. Do you know what a winger is?"

"A wide attacker?"

“More or less. It does not mean ‘stand next to the touchline and wait for the ball.’ Your job changes depending on what the fullback does.”

Mercer pointed behind Taiga.

“Bennett. Show him.”

A current player stepped into the deeper wide position. Mercer continued.

“That is the fullback. Defender first. When we have the ball, he can come forward outside you. That run is an overlap.”

Bennett jogged past the mannequin to demonstrate. As he passed Taiga, he said, “Try not to run into me.”

“Then stay out of my way.”

Bennett raised an eyebrow. Then he snorted and kept moving. Mercer moved Taiga two steps inward.

“If he goes outside, you usually do not occupy the same strip of grass. Come inside. Make the defender choose. Follow you and the overlap is free. Stay with the overlap and you can receive inside.”

Taiga looked at the shape. That made sense. Mercer pointed at the gap that had opened between two defenders.

“This is the part that matters. If Bennett makes one defender move, somebody has to notice what he left behind.”

Taiga nodded.

“I guess I don't always have to move inside.”

“Right. Soccer would be much easier if every answer was always.” Mercer stepped back. “Read the defender. Read your fullback. Read where the ball is. The diagram gives you options, not permission to stop thinking.”

Then he proceeded with the others. After assigning each of them a role, Mercer and his assistants divided them in four groups, mixing current players and candidates. He saw the blond guy, Leo, going with the candidates who wanted to play in attack.
Bennet was in his group. 

Then the assistant made them position in formation and move to test the basics.

They started. First repetition: Bennett went. Taiga stayed wide. Whistle.

“Akatora.”

“Yeah. Inside.”

“Then go there before I have to tell you.”

Reset. Second repetition, Taiga moved too early and dragged himself into the central midfielder's lane. The assistants stopped them again.

“Different problem. Same cause.”

Taiga frowned.

“I moved.”

“You moved because you remembered a rule. You need to move because you saw something.”

Heat climbed his neck. Third repetition. He checked over his shoulder first. Bennett starting high. Defender watching him. Central midfielder already occupying the inside lane. Taiga held width for another second. Then the midfielder dropped, the lane opened, and Taiga stepped into it. 
Pass inside. One touch. Bennett around him. Return ball outside.
Clean. 
Mercer appeared on his side and said, “Better,” and was already resetting the drill.
Good. 
They ran it from the other side and everything became backwards.

Taiga wanted to murder geometry. 

Later came pressing. That word he knew. The details were worse.

Mercer collapsed all groups in two teams on the whole pitch.

“You do not sprint at every person who receives it. Pressing is coordinated pressure. If Akatora goes and the rest of you stay home, Akatora is not pressing. He is jogging angrily by himself.”

Someone behind Taiga laughed. He turned: Mendez was grinning openly.
Taiga glared at him. 
He pressed his lips together with exaggerated effort.

“Sorry. Mercer just described your entire personality as a tactical error.”

“Keep smiling and I’ll show you something you missed.”

“That sounded affectionate.”

Mercer blew the whistle before Taiga could answer.

Reading when to move and when not to was way harder than Taiga had anticipated.
By the sixth repetition Taiga could feel the structure rather than recite it. 
Then the scrimmage began and the structure immediately became less polite. Players moved before he expected them to. Passing lanes existed for half a second. A correct position became wrong when somebody else made a different decision. He lost the ball six times. Won it five. Created one assist after beating the right-back and cutting the ball toward Price, the striker, who finished first time.
Price pointed at Taiga while jogging back.

“Do that again.”

Taiga almost told him to fuck off. Then realized it was probably praise. He missed an easy shot ten minutes later, sending it straight at the goalkeeper Mercer had called Pierce. Pierce caught it without moving, then shouted:

“WALSH, STEP\! BENNETT, TUCK IN\!”

Then, quieter as Taiga ran past, “Far corner was open.”

Taiga intended to remember both the miss and the comment at three in the morning for the next decade. 

The tryout lasted almost two hours. Eventually, Mercer gathered everybody at midfield.

"That's all. Thank you for coming. Final selections will be emailed tomorrow in the afternoon."

That was it. Taiga picked up his bag with his stomach tied into a knot.
Waiting was worse than failure. Failure, at least, was information.

Tuesday morning he had his first Foundations of Computer Science lecture. That was the class Taiga was looking for the most. 
He had never been a computer nerd, but during last summer Hyunjin showed him enough coding to make him interested.

Since then he, had messed with some basic Git, JavaScript and even a bit of Java. 
He liked it. Computers don't argue. Or at least, that's what he had been thinking until the class.

Professor Rao took less then half an hour to destroy every certainty Taiga had. 
Nothing he had learned showed up during the lecture.
Instead, Rao talked about algorithms, complexity and computational cost.

Then he described the basics of variables and loops. That part, Taiga liked it better.

That afternoon, Taiga was trying hard to focus on the readings for Political Institutions. 
Every time his phone buzzed, he jumped at it, only to be disappointed with a useless notification.

By 4:15, he hadn't yet gone through the first half of the first reading.

Caleb looked across the room. “Are you waiting for something?”

“No.”

“You've refreshed your email twelve times.”

Taiga turned. “You were counting?”

“The page flashes white every time.”

“That is somehow worse.”

The Camp Buddy chat was already flooded with them asking for news.

2.43 **Keitaro:** Any news Taiga?  

3.01 **Hiro:** IT IS AFTER THREE GIVE US NEWS  

3.32 **Natsumi:** Any word yet?  

3.47 **Hunter:** No pressure. But also yes, some pressure.  

4.05 **Yoichi:** dynamite answer ur fucking phone

At some point, he had silenced them and put the phone face down on the desk.

"I'm waiting for soccer results."

"For the tryouts?"

"Yeah."

Caleb stopped a second.

"If they don't take you, will they still notify you?"

Taiga froze. He hadn't thought about that.
He looked at the time: 4.35
He felt suddenly sick.

A fight started immediately in his brain. 4.35 was still early enough to not jump at any conclusion. But it was also late enough to justify worrying.

"I don't know."

"I think they probably will."

"Why?"

"Because it would be unnecessarily cruel not to. And if nobody was selected, then you could think of a system failure."

Taiga considered. It made enough sense to slightly loosen the knot inside.

He looked at the reading opened on his laptop. He rubbed his face with his hands.
That was stupid.
There was no point in stop functioning until a fucking email arrived.

He had managed to get almost to the ending of the readings when a small notification appeared on the top right of his screen. A new mail.

**Olympus University Athletics**  
**Men's Soccer — Freshman Selection**

Everything stopped. He stared at it for half a minute. Then he opened it.

**We are pleased to offer you a developmental roster position with the Olympus University Men's Soccer Club for the upcoming semester.**

He read the sentence twice. 
Developmental. Not first squad. 
Training access, reserve selection, a six-week review. 

"Fuck me."

He heard Caleb glancing over.

"Oh. That looks like good news."

Taiga didn't answer immediately.
His phone buzzed again. Keitaro, private message.

**Keitaro:** Taiga? Are you alright?

Taiga stared at it. Then he opened Camp Buddy group chat and sent him a picture of the mail.

The phone exploded.

**Hiro:** HOLY SHIT  
**Keitaro:** TAIGA\!\!\!\! ❤️  
**Natsumi:** CONGRATULATIONS\!\!\!  
**Hunter:** That's amazing. Seriously.  
**Eduard:** I KNEW IIIIIIT ❤️❤️❤️❤️❤️❤️.  
**Lee:** That was an obvious outcome. Your physical prowess and strategic mind have been recognized.  
**Yoichi:** knew it

Keitaro started a video call. Taiga answered before it could ring twice.

“You got in\!”

“Just developmental roster.”

Keitaro laughed.

“There it is.”

“I was explaining.”

“You were minimizing. What does developmental mean?”

“I train with them. Reserve matches if selected. Review after six weeks.”

“So you're on the team.”

“Sort of.”

“Taiga.”

Hiro shoved into frame. “He is literally on the roster.”

More squares joined while they argued: Hunter from an art studio, Natsumi outside a library, Yoichi from somewhere with a ceiling and no useful camera angle, Eduard with one eye finished in eyeliner, Lee beside him with a textbook open.

“Before anyone comments,” Eduard said, “yes, I interrupted my face for this.”

Lee adjusted his glasses. 

“You had already interrupted it because you could not choose between two products.”

“Fine, Lee. I interrupted deciding which one to use.”

Taiga smiled.

Hunter said, “Even if it's developmental, you've basically never played serious soccer. That's impressive.”

“Maybe they needed people.”

Natsumi laughed.

“Teams are allowed to reject people they don't want.”

“Groundbreaking.”

Keitaro watched him with the expression Taiga knew too well. 

“You tried something you were convinced you had no business doing, and they asked you to stay. You can let that be good news for one night.”

Across the room, Caleb said, “I told him that.”

Everyone went quiet.

Taiga turned. “You weren't invited.”

Caleb looked up from his book. “I wasn't joining the call.”

Hiro's face lit up. 

“IS THAT CALEB?”

Taiga sighed.

"Yeah."

Caleb asked:

"Is that your scout group?"

"Unfortunately."

Hiro shouted again.

"CALEB COME IN WE WANT TO SEE YOU."

Caleb looked at Taiga. Taiga sighed again and turned the phone to include him.

He waved awkwardly.

"Hello."

They spent the next five minutes introducing everyone to him.

Eventually the conversation drifted away. Hunter had studio work. Natsumi had a group assignment. Hiro had a lecturer at war with readable slides. Yoichi was calling from Camp Buddy after spending most of the afternoon somewhere in the woods with Yuki. Eduard and Lee argued about something cosmetic that became, somehow, an argument about evidence. 

People left one at a time. Keitaro stayed last. Obviously.
Taiga looked at him.

"What."

Keitaro just looked at him, smiling.

"I'll never say it you know."

Keitaro laughed.

“Call me after if you want to complain.”

“That I can do.”

Taiga looked at the roster email. “Keitaro.”

“Yeah?”

“...Thanks.”

Keitaro did not make him repeat it. “Anytime.”

After the call ended, Taiga reread the offer.
A familiar feeling suddenly awoke.

Two months earlier, when the Olympus scholarship email arrived at Camp Buddy, his first response had been:

They made a mistake.

Yoshinori had put one hand on his shoulder and said, 

“Then perhaps you should give them the opportunity to discover it themselves.”

Taiga had hated that answer. It had also worked.
Developmental roster. Not first squad. Not guaranteed. 
But it was still something.

Across the room, Caleb turned a page.

“You look less tense.”

Taiga glanced down. His leg had stopped bouncing.

“You notice too much.”

“I've been told that.”

“By Maya?”

“Mostly my mother.”

Taiga smiled faintly. The campus still felt too big and too loud, but for the first time since arriving, one place on the map felt slightly less theoretical.

The following morning started with the Political Institution again. 

Taiga arrived eight minutes early and took the same seat as Monday. Third row from the back. Near enough to see. Far enough that nobody would mistake his existence for enthusiasm.

The girl who had sat beside him last time arrived a minute later.

Dark hair tied up, glasses, coffee in one hand. The assigned book was tucked under her arm, with lots of colored tabs sticking out of it. 
She stopped beside him.

"Is this taken?"

Taiga shook his head.

She sat and immediately opened her book. Taiga was checking some passages from the readings.

"You actually did the readings?"

Taiga turned.

"Why?"

She shrugged. 

"It was 87 pages. Not everybody does it."

Taiga raised an eyebrow. She continued:

"Can I ask a question then?"

"You sound like you want to interrogate me."

She smiled.

"No, no. But since you read them, I want you opinion on something."

"Still sounds like an interrogation."

"Then say no."

Taiga sighed. 

"What?"

She tapped the page.

“This. Do you think that actually counts as a choice?”

Taiga leaned over enough to read the highlighted sentence. It was something about people accepting political authority by continuing to live under it.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because leaving is actually pretty hard."

"But technically, it's still a choice."

"When staying is the only realistic decision you can make, can you really say it's a choice?"

"It's forced, but still a choice."

“That's a stupid definition of choice.”

“No, it's an inconvenient definition of choice.”

Taiga stared at her. She looked completely serious.
Before he could reply, Professor Hall walked in.

He started where they had left off. This time Taiga could actually follow without opening three tabs and wondering what the hell everyone else knew that he didn't.

About twenty minutes in, Hall returned to the same section the girl had shown him.

A student in the front row raised his hand.

“If someone really rejects the system they're living under, can't they just leave?”

Taiga looked up. Seriously? 

The girl crossed her arms, listening.
Front Row Guy kept talking.

“If people stay and keep following the rules, at some point that has to mean they accept them.”

Taiga raised his hand before thinking too much about it.

Hall looked toward him.

“Yes?”

Taiga became painfully aware of everyone behind him. 
He took a breath and said:

“If leaving means giving up your job, your home, your family and basically your entire life, then staying doesn't prove you agree with anything.”

Hall nodded.

“So what does it prove?”

Taiga paused.

“Mostly that leaving would suck.”

A few people laughed.

“Less academically phrased than I would prefer, but yes.”

Taiga leaned back. Good enough.
Then the girl beside him raised her hand.

Hall pointed at her.

“But staying still tells us something,” she said. “If people keep following rules even when nobody is forcing them every single time, that matters. It just doesn't tell us why they're doing it.”

Hall nodded again.

“Exactly. Behaviour is evidence. It isn't an explanation by itself.”

He wrote something on the board and continued.
Taiga glanced at her.

“You really had to add that?”

She glanced over.

“You made it sound completely useless.”

“It mostly is.”

“No, it isn't.”

“Here we go.”

“Those are two different questions.”

Taiga stared at her.

“Do you talk like this all the time?”

“Like what?”

“Like there's going to be a test on the conversation.”

She frowned.

“There probably will be a test on this conversation.”

Taiga laughed before he could stop himself.
The girl smiled as well.

“I'm Nora, by the way.”

“Taiga.”

“Nice to meet you.”

“Sure.”

She looked over.

“That's a weird response.”

Taiga shrugged.

“You'll survive.”

By the end of class, Taiga had three pages of notes, two more chapters to read and apparently another annoying figure in his academic life.

As everyone started packing, Nora closed her book.

“I still think staying counts as a choice.”

Taiga put his laptop in his bag.

“And I still think that's stupid.”

“Good.”

He looked at her.

“Good?”

“It would've been boring if you changed your mind already.”

Then she picked up her coffee and left. Taiga watched her disappear into the aisle.

Maybe this class wouldn't be completely terrible.
