Home/Away — Act I narrative and prose review
20 October 2026

Read: all twelve current final chapters, approximately 55,000 words, at repository commit 75fc4a8d81926cc16d12c60b1a561dd7aecf24f7. This is a review, not an edit: no manuscript or bible files were changed.

I read all ten Final Bibles and consulted the relevant Act I material in the academic, calendar, character, Leo-family, romance, match, writing, Camp Buddy canon and Scoutmaster Season references. The current final manuscript is the basis of the narrative assessment. Older plans are identified as older plans rather than used to manufacture manuscript errors. Later-act plans establish whether a thread is deliberately deferred; they do not prove that its eventual payoff works.

**Overall judgment**

Act I works as a continuous coming-of-age arc. It has a recognizable beginning, a meaningful intermediate resolution and a different question at its end. Taiga arrives treating Olympus as a possible administrative mistake; he leaves for the holidays expecting to return to a life that is his. Romance develops inside that larger change.

The act does not need another romantic beat, a new crisis, a more dramatic first start or a redistribution that gives everybody a subplot. Its main weaknesses are localized: some conversations explain a change after other scenes have already demonstrated it; the Halloween plan exposes its machinery; several supporting characters are defined more narrowly in action than their bibles suggest; and a few concrete continuity errors survived the chapter-by-chapter passes.

The most useful revisions would be:

1. Reconcile the autumn calendar with the 2025–26 year and correct the smaller factual/staging slips.
2. Add a minimal outcome for Aaron and Sofia's date in the November material.
3. Shorten the explanation that forces Taiga and Leo into the same Halloween subgroup.
4. Give Zoe one small instance of visible competence within an existing scene.
5. Reduce repeated explanatory work in the major-declaration and late Camp Buddy conversations.
6. Distinguish Chapter 10's closing choreography from Chapter 8's.
7. Make a selective prose pass, especially for reaction beats, awkward idioms and duplicated sentences.

These are not equal-severity findings. The calendar is an error; Zoe's balance and the ending variation are editorial recommendations.

The strongest structural decision is that Chapter 6 resolves retention without resolving the whole act. After that, the question becomes what Taiga does with his place, rather than whether another authority will permit him to keep it.

The main pacing vulnerability is the repeated shape of some scenes: an invitation or question, Taiga's rejection, someone pointing out what his behavior already means, reluctant concession, sarcastic button. This belongs to his personality, but when it appears in academic, social and romantic scenes close together, the mechanism can become more predictable than the individual event.

Do not remove his resistance wholesale. Let some later scenes begin after he has already agreed. Let the disagreement concern something specific rather than whether he will participate at all. Chapter 11's private declaration and Chapter 12's straightforward acceptance of the outing already demonstrate that change.

**2. Taiga: consistent development, with one area to protect**

His progression is convincing because his capacities change at different speeds.

- He can regulate an impulse in Chapter 1, yet still lose control during Chapter 3 practice.
- He apologizes to Evan without needing a long moral lesson.
- With Caleb, he can recognize a reasonable motive while maintaining the boundary about his belongings.
- He improves at football while remaining vulnerable to institutional evaluation.
- By Chapter 6 he catches the “still under ninety” response himself.
- At Arcadia he loses repeatedly and keeps playing. That is an especially useful contrast with the earlier practice spiral: competition is becoming something he can enjoy when his place is not at stake.
- He gives Aaron imperfect advice and then discovers that the same advice applies to his own major declaration.
- He remains blunt and profane at the end, but much of the hostility has become shared play.

The social change is also present in what he notices: Caleb's discomfort at the party, Maya's work, Hunter's nerves, Nora's dog, Leo's tiredness and finally the absence of Caleb's ordinary things.

The scholarship does not need another concluding speech. “One point for Olympus,” the review, the major declaration and the final room already answer its emotional question in stages.

The risk is making too many people exceptionally good at interpreting him. Keitaro, Nora, Caleb, Maya, Mercer, Park, Evan and Leo all have scenes in which they clarify some aspect of his resistance. Most have legitimate reasons to do so. Cumulatively, however, the world can feel unusually well equipped to facilitate his development. The answer is selective compression and occasional noninterpretive reactions, not making friends less kind.

**Zoe's specific problem**

She needs Taiga to restore a branch in Chapter 4, brings another Git problem to dinner in Chapter 5, and receives a full rescue in Chapter 6. The cumulative image is not just chaotic organization: it is technical dependence. Her bible describes a competent pragmatist, but much of that competence currently exists off-page.

This can be fixed without adding a scene. After Taiga restores the history, let her immediately identify or solve the application-level issue; let her explain an actual intended behavior he has misunderstood; or let the text show that her messy project does something worthwhile. She can be bad at Git and good at programming. The reader needs one visible distinction.

Her rescue of Leo's sleeve, refusal to accept his volunteered use of Taiga's time, and relaxed social relationships already give her other forms of agency. Preserve those.

**Enzo's specific problem**

Chapter 8's train conversation tells us he knows when to say the right thing or shut up. Chapter 10 repeatedly makes everyone agree that he must be managed, removed or physically silenced. Those can coexist, but the second version dominates the evidence.

Do not add a solemn confession to prove he has depth. A brief change of behavior when a joke actually hurts Aaron, or an unannounced practical accommodation for Leo, would be enough. He already accepts Taiga's refusal of the Chapter 3 dinner; that is a useful quieter precedent.

**The best nonprotagonist chemistry**

Caleb/Maya works because affection changes the communication without erasing their differences. Her “celebrating my eighty-eight” boundary is particularly good: she gets to want something, and he can respond.

Evan/Taiga works because the early conflict is repaired and later advice has earned permission.

Gav/Enzo works through incompatible investments: Enzo wants a performance; Gav cares sincerely about the thing under discussion. Roberto makes that opposition concrete.

Aaron/Sofia works best when she acts, rather than when the men discuss her. The basketball rematch and soda invitation make her a participant.

Leo/Enzo has credible shared history in the school, team and family exchanges. Their similarity in volume does not erase the relationship.

Leo/Maya's immediate Wicked conversation is an effective crossing of social circles because it has a specific shared interest. Hunter/Keitaro's photography exchange is similarly valuable: the supporting cast are allowed to have relationships that do not need Taiga to interpret them.

**Voice overlap to watch**

The ensemble is differentiated, but several characters borrow the same quick diagnostic style. “It was an observation” belongs naturally to Caleb and Lee; the difference is Caleb's economy versus Lee's elaborate wording. Nora, Zoe and Maya should not all become equally good at naming Taiga's motives in a single clean sentence.

This is a matter of selective lines, not a need to redesign their voices.

**5. Cohesion, exposition and visible construction**

The act is more connected than its number of scene breaks suggests. Several threads cross domains:

- Football feedback and Hall's feedback teach related skills without being identical subjects.
- Taiga's camp experience becomes useful in the clinic and on the hike.
- Git brings Zoe and Leo into his academic life and then brings Nora into their table.
- Photos connect Nora to the hike, Leo to camp, the team to the old friends, and Taiga and Leo to a shared Halloween memory.
- The costume physically moves from the old home into the new one.
- Aaron's difficulty acting becomes the basis of Taiga's own declaration.
- Room 317 moves from unfamiliar space to negotiated shared space to a home whose missing occupant is noticeable.

These are substantial connective devices. The act does not read like twelve unrelated episodes.

The potential staccato effect is mostly local: short dialogue lines separated by looks, pauses and reactions, followed by a scene break and another conversation with the same rhythm. Some of that should be addressed within scenes rather than by adding transitions between chapters.

**The most visibly engineered passage: the Halloween splitting plan**

Chapter 10's comic premise is sound: the friends turn a simple invitation into an absurd military operation. The point where the construction becomes too visible is the extended argument explaining why Taiga and Leo, specifically, must leave together:

“Why can't I just go with Caleb and Maya and Leo goes with you?”

The reply then explains why the entire soccer team cannot leave at once, and Taiga concludes that this “made perfect sense.”

That is more justification than the situation needs. It sounds as though the story is defending its need for a two-person outing. The plan is also conspicuously elaborate even though Sofia has every opportunity to notice what is happening.

Recommendation: keep the ridiculous mission, the obvious bad acting and the group thinning. Shorten the pairing debate. Taiga already studies with Leo; the audience does not need every alternative configuration disproved.

The escape itself is better than the explanation: the old costume photograph embarrasses Taiga, he goes for food, Leo follows, and Leo admits he did not plan it that cleverly. Keep that causality.

**The major-declaration sequence: strong payoff, too many explanations around it**

The declaration is well prepared. Taiga likes coding, helps people with it, chooses future CS courses, keeps the page open, and ultimately acts without needing public congratulations.

The risk lies in how often somebody supplies the same permissive idea:

- Caleb: changing majors is not free, but not irreversible.
- Park: a direction is not a lifetime sentence.
- Nora: the chosen courses already look like a CS major.
- Evan: he does not know his post-graduation path.
- Leo: liking a school project was enough reason to try CS, and declaring does not mean absolute certainty.

These are not all redundant. Park provides useful institutional information; Evan demonstrates uncertainty at a later life stage; Leo provides a peer's version. But Chapter 11's Leo exchange restates much of what Taiga explicitly says he does not need another conversation about.

Keep the debugging, Leo's simple reason for liking CS, and Taiga applying his own Aaron advice. Compress the additional discussion about what other undeclared students study and what declaring does or does not mean. The portal action will carry more weight with less argumentative preparation immediately before it.

Park's office scene is credible because advice is her job. Her last “being unsure doesn't make the interest fake” line is optional: the useful advising work has already happened. One neat departing aphorism is not a problem; several adults producing them across an act can become a pattern.

**The late Camp Buddy conversation: earned intimacy, dense institutional history**

Chapter 8 establishes the outline: three summers, a bad second term, the off-season rebuilding and a fuller third summer. Chapter 12 earns a deeper version by revealing the guilt underneath Taiga's decision to stay and the difficulty of choosing to leave.

The new emotional information is valuable. The lower-value stretch is the sponsor/publisher sequence: who funded the expansion, how the journal was published, Clermont's reputation, the royalties, the recommendation and the application.

Each question is plausible individually. Together they briefly turn Leo into a guide through the canon reference.

Recommendation: preserve why Taiga stayed, why leaving was difficult and what Olympus changed. Compress the financial/publication chain. Do not remove the conversation or make Taiga confess the burned-journal history yet.

The two interrupted disclosures — “It was a shame that I—” and later “I fucked up a thing or two” — make withheld history visible. The second, partial admission is more emotionally informative. Consider whether the first cutoff is needed as an additional mystery signal, especially after the interrupted history with Aaron in Chapter 10.

**The Spanish exposition**

The mother call, scarf and flight anxiety in Chapter 12 are organic: information arrives through an interruption and a physical object. Taiga asking afterward about language with Enzo is credible.

The weaker setup is Chapter 11's detour through Spanish being widely spoken and Taiga's surprise, immediately after Evan names his degree. It does little for Evan's uncertainty and makes Taiga ask conspicuously elementary questions so the reader can receive background information.

Keep Evan's Modern Languages major and uncertain future. The Spanish statistics and explanatory chain can be shortened; Chapter 12 already has a stronger mechanism for that material.

**Tarot**

The stall fits Halloween and gives Taiga and Leo something to do. Its exact offer → self-imposed obstacle → future conflict progression, followed by a polished prediction, is one of the more author-shaped moments.

This is not a missing Act I payoff. The chapter map explicitly intends later resonance. The issue is tone and emphasis now. Both boys leave “a bit preoccupied,” which gives the prediction more authority than a casual party reading necessarily needs.

Keep it if a real superstition or ominous thread is intended. Otherwise let the reaction remain more provisional, amused or skeptical, and avoid emphasizing the forecast again before the later events earn the resemblance. It does not need a token argument inserted into Act I to fulfil it.

**Other constructed moments that remain justified**

Yuri's bag is convenient, but it fits an already nosy, inventive adult and is introduced before its contents are revealed. It also creates a choice: Taiga adapts the costume rather than simply receiving a complete solution. No additional explanatory scene is necessary.

The repeated costume appearances are mostly justified by different audiences: the reader sees the adaptation through Maya/Caleb; Leo recognizes what he saw in the photo; the group photograph discussion helps produce the escape; the old friends recognize their own shared past. The repeated admiration can be trimmed, but these are not five interchangeable reveals.

The rink collision is conventional, but the prolonged handholding has already begun the emotional change. Keep the earlier physical beat so the accident does not become a magical origin for feelings.

**8. Continuity: factual errors, ambiguities and reference drift**

**C1 — Calendar conflict: definite, high priority.**

The Master Calendar declares the academic year 2025–26. Its December anchors, Friday December 12 and Saturday December 13, fit 2025.

The late-October sequence currently uses:

- Chapter 7: “thursday the 29th” for the clinic.
- Chapter 9: exhibit the next day; return on an early Saturday train for the party.
- Chapter 10: “Saturday evening” for Halloween.
- Chapter 5: exhibition explicitly on October 30.

Those weekdays fit October 2026. In 2025, October 29 is Wednesday, October 30 Thursday and October 31 Friday. The chapter map and calendar have copied the inconsistent sequence rather than resolved it.

Choose one coherent calendar before changing prose. Keeping the 2025–26 academic year offers two reasonable routes: keep Halloween on Friday October 31 and move the clinic/exhibit weekday wording accordingly; or hold the campus celebration on Saturday November 1 and shift the preceding event dates, explicitly treating it as that weekend's Halloween celebration. Do not simply change the whole novel to 2026–27: later locked dates depend on the original year.

**C2 — Arrival/tryout calendar: definite reference mismatch.**

Final Chapter 1 has the first tryout on Thursday and first-cut results on Friday. Final Chapter 2 has callback Monday and the roster offer Tuesday. The Master Calendar still puts first tryout Tuesday August 26, callback Wednesday August 27 and the offer Friday August 29.

Update the reference to the final sequence. Leo says he saw Taiga watching “yesterday,” so the arrival/practice-watching scene is implied to be Wednesday unless that one word changes. The opening orientation app also needs to agree with the selected arrival day.

A coherent working option within 2025 is arrival Wednesday August 27, tryout Thursday August 28, first cut Friday August 29, callback Monday September 1, offer Tuesday September 2; the October weekday problem still needs its separate decision. This is a proposed reconciliation, not an existing fact.

**C3 — Chapter 1 orientation greeting: definite.**

Orientation begins at three; Avan opens with “Good morning.” Change the greeting to afternoon, unless the mismatch is intentionally a joke and the text acknowledges it.

**C4 — Chapter 6's lab instructor title: definite.**

Chapter 4 establishes Seth Miller as a graduate assistant; Chapter 6 calls him “Professor Seth.” Use Seth or the established graduate-assistant designation. The academic bible agrees with Chapter 4.

**C5 — Evan's account of Mercer's recruitment: an ambiguity with structural consequences.**

Chapter 11 says Mercer “could have picked another winger and kept you in development for the whole year or as a reserve.” Taiga is currently in development and remains there. This can mean Mercer could have blocked his route to succession, but the wording also implies development was a path Mercer chose not to take.

Clarify future opportunity versus present status. Keep Evan's account visibly his reading of Mercer's intentions, not a guaranteed succession plan. His later “not donating you my job” line helps, but it does not entirely untangle the earlier wording.

**C6 — Chapter 9's noon timing: check, not a proven contradiction.**

Campus appears “a little before noon,” Taiga walks to the room, lies down “maybe half an hour,” opens the bag, then sees 12:06. This requires a sufficiently early arrival and short walk. It is possible, but the precision can make readers calculate something the scene does not need. If editing the timing anyway, align the approximate rest with the exact clock or remove one of the unnecessary measurements.

**C7 — Final-bible Northbridge entry: definite stale reference.**

The Revision Ledger still says Chapter 11 returns to Northbridge for the first start. Final Chapter 11 and the updated Apollo Team/Chapter Map correctly say Briarwood. Update the ledger forward; do not change the final match back to Northbridge.

**9. Prose, narration and voice**

The narrative voice is recognizably Taiga's throughout. It attends to practical irritations, competition, awkward social demands, objects and people before it can explain feelings. The early sensory overload, his irritation at advice, the timetable, the shirt and his final response to the room all belong to the same person.

His later warmth is not a voice break. What changes is the meaning of the irritation and what he chooses to do despite it. Do not make later narration more hostile simply to reproduce Chapter 1's surface.

**Fragments and imperfect English**

Keep deliberate fragments such as “No. Definitely not.” and “Starting. Actual fucking starting.” They carry perception, emphasis and emotional rhythm. Contractions, swearing, casual message spelling, false starts and uneven speech are part of the cast.

The distinction is between a productive irregularity and a construction that accidentally changes meaning. “St. Vincent fullback followed him” loses an article without gaining character; “waited too much” is an awkward duration expression; “went back to eat” describes a different action from resuming a meal. These can be corrected without making Taiga eloquent.

Some figurative oddities, such as “metabolizing the thought,” are understandable and can be kept if you like their flavor. They do not belong in the same severity category as a typo.

**The dominant repetition is choreography**

These are diagnostic counts, not prohibited-word quotas. A dialogue-heavy novel will contain looks and smiles. The issue is clustering: a look, a blink, a pause, a question, another look, then a shrug can choreograph every tiny turn of an exchange without changing what the reader understands.

The best correction is often deletion. Replacing “looked” with “gazed,” “regarded” or “studied” would merely decorate the same beat.

Retain physical beats that establish who is where, make the timing funny, show a meaningful hesitation or carry something the dialogue does not. The mother's call, scarf, continued handholding and final elevator hesitation are doing that work. Many ordinary conversational looks do not.

**Three particularly useful repetition passes**

1. **Chapter 3's “worse” commentary.** The chapter contains “That made everything worse,” “That was somehow worse,” “which somehow made it worse,” and “That made it worse,” as well as nearby variations. The events differ, but the commentary keeps delivering the same judgment. Keep the strongest instance or two; let the other situations demonstrate the escalation.
2. **Involuntary laughter and smiles in Chapters 2–3.** “Before he could stop himself” and “despite himself” recur around small positive reactions. Early resistance matters, but repeated framing makes every laugh feel like the same tiny breach. Later chapters already allow him ordinary enjoyment; preserve that progression rather than marking every smile as a defeat.
3. **Chapter 12's opening recap.** “A few days before finals week” is followed immediately by “In the days before finals.” Enzo's spreadsheet turning eleven slash eleven into a date is then explained once in the common-room overview and again in the next paragraph. Merge each pair. This is straightforward repetition without an additional payoff.

Also watch the reusable exchange “What?” / “Nothing,” and the expression → Taiga anticipates a joke → denial or shrug pattern. They are available tools, but should not become the default bridge between meaningful lines.

The narration's hostile personification belongs to Taiga: technology, schedules and environments can feel as if they are acting against him. Keep the images that arise from the particular object or problem. Generic variations on war, crime, betrayal and personal offense become interchangeable if every scene receives one.

**POV boundaries**

There are two clear, easy-to-fix slips:

- Chapter 1 names “Leo” in narration immediately before he introduces himself as Leo. Taiga has heard “Mendez,” so using Mendez or a description for that one sentence keeps the introduction inside his knowledge.
- Chapter 10 says Sofia is talking to “another girl no one knew.” Taiga can report whom he recognizes; he cannot establish that none of the group knows her.

The ordinary interpretations of expressions are generally legitimate close-third inferences. Do not attach “seemed” or “apparently” to every feeling merely to avoid an omniscience accusation. Reserve qualification for something he cannot reasonably observe or infer.

**Narrative rhythm and screenplay mechanics**

The act's short paragraphs suit its conversational emphasis. Some exchanges, however, have effectively become a sequence of stage directions between lines of dialogue. That is the prose-level source of the staccato impression.

Where a scene becomes choppy, combine a few actions into a continuous sentence or paragraph organized around Taiga's attention. Do not add scenic description just to slow the pace. A setting detail earns space when it affects attention, movement, privacy, discomfort or the conversation.

Crowded scenes work best when characters have different immediate activities. Halloween occasionally turns the ensemble into a row of perfectly timed replies; Arcadia and the exhibition have stronger task-based differentiation. Preserve basketball, games, costume maintenance, food and photographs as actual activities, rather than treating them only as backgrounds for dialogue.

There is no need to dramatize every match or class. The selection of scenes is sensible. A brief date outcome is needed because that event was strongly promised; an ordinary unshown week is not a continuity gap.
