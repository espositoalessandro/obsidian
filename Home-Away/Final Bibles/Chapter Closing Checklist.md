# Home/Away — Chapter Closing Checklist

This file defines the **final chapter-closing audit** for the rewrite.

Use it after the chapter's structure is substantially complete and before declaring the chapter frozen.

The order matters:

> **structure → continuity → character/POV → scene craft → prose → grammar → freeze**

Do not spend time polishing sentences that may still be cut or moved.

---

# 1. Chapter Job

Before line editing, state in one sentence what changes between the beginning and end of the chapter.

Check:

- Does the chapter actually accomplish that movement?
- Does every major scene contribute to it?
- Is any scene present mainly because it existed in the Draft?
- Is anything important missing because the rewrite compressed too aggressively?
- Does the chapter end when its dramatic unit ends rather than where the old Draft chapter ended?

---

# 2. Scene Ladder

For every scene, identify:

- what Taiga enters wanting, expecting or trying to do;
- what happens;
- what is different when he leaves.

Then check:

- Does the scene introduce information, relationship movement, conflict, character texture or setup?
- Are two scenes doing essentially the same job?
- Does any scene continue after its useful work is finished?
- Are scene breaks genuinely needed?
- Are continuous events being artificially chopped into mini-scenes?

---

# 3. Continuity With Previous Final Chapters

Check concrete continuity before polishing prose.

Audit:

- dates;
- weekdays;
- time of day;
- class schedule;
- football schedule;
- roster status;
- who has met whom;
- what names Taiga currently knows;
- what information Taiga currently knows;
- locations;
- travel time;
- clothing or equipment where relevant;
- room arrangements;
- relationship state;
- injuries;
- assignments;
- promises;
- plans;
- ongoing conversations.

Make sure nobody reacts as though something has already happened when it has not.

---

# 4. Future-Continuity Dependency

Check the completed Draft for later material that depends on this chapter.

Ask:

- Did the rewrite accidentally remove setup for a later event?
- Did it introduce something that contradicts later canon?
- Did it move a reveal too early?
- Did it make a relationship noticeably closer or colder than later chapters assume?
- Did it remove an object, routine, promise or fact that later scenes rely on?

Where possible, solve future tensions **forward** rather than reopening frozen chapters.

---

# 5. Final Bible Compliance

Check the relevant Final Bibles.

Ask:

- Does the chapter follow current character and voice targets?
- Has a deliberate Final-Bible change overridden old Draft canon?
- Is the rewrite accidentally restoring something that was intentionally changed?
- Has anything new emerged that is genuinely global enough to add to a Final Bible?
- Is a supposed “rule” actually only a local scene choice?

Do not update a bible merely because one scene made a local decision.

---

# 6. Taiga POV

The narrative camera belongs to Taiga.

Check:

- Is every narrative observation plausibly selected or framed by Taiga?
- Is another character's internal state being reported as fact?
- Does description focus on what Taiga would actually notice?
- Is his bias present without forcing sarcasm into every paragraph?
- Does his current familiarity with Olympus match his development?
- Is the narration accidentally resetting him to an earlier version of himself?
- Does his attention increasingly reflect the people and routines he now knows?

---

# 7. Character Progression

Check that development accumulates rather than resets.

Ask:

- Does Taiga move by an appropriate amount?
- Is the change too large for one chapter?
- Is there effectively no movement at all?
- Does the chapter preserve development from earlier chapters?
- If Taiga regresses, is there a clear reason?
- Are positive observations allowed to exist when appropriate?
- Are relationships changing his behavior rather than merely being explained by narration?

---

# 8. Character Voices

Check recurring characters against their Final Bible voices.

Ask:

- Could the recurring characters be identified without dialogue tags?
- Is each person's humor coming from their own mechanism?
- Is anyone drifting into generic dry sarcasm?
- Is Leo energetic and socially expansive rather than therapist-like?
- Is Caleb precise rather than robotic or emotionally diagnostic?
- Is Nora argumentative rather than generically clever?
- Are Apollo characters behaving according to their established identities?
- Does serious behavior differ appropriately from comic/default behavior?

---

# 9. Dialogue

Read dialogue as speech rather than information delivery.

Check:

- Would a real person actually say this aloud?
- Is dialogue explaining information both characters already know?
- Is somebody verbalizing the theme?
- Are emotional conversations too explicit?
- Could a line be replaced by silence, hesitation, interruption or action?
- Are dialogue beats placed where timing or staging actually needs them?
- Are too many beats interrupting otherwise clear exchanges?
- Do arguments evolve rather than merely alternate opposing statements?

---

# 10. Emotional Causality

The reader should understand emotional movement without being over-explained.

Check:

- Can the reader tell why Taiga reacts the way he does?
- Has the scene earned the intensity of the reaction?
- Is narration explaining an emotion already visible in behavior?
- Is a major reaction happening without enough setup?
- Do emotional shifts occur through actual beats rather than a sentence announcing the change?

---

# 11. Physical Staging

Make the scene physically legible.

Check:

- Where is everyone?
- Who is facing whom?
- What are their bodies and hands doing when it matters?
- Can the reader follow entrances and exits?
- Can the reader follow movement during football or action?
- Do objects appear or disappear without explanation?
- Are characters performing impossible simultaneous actions?
- Is the staging clear without over-choreographing every gesture?

---

# 12. Football Check

Use this whenever football appears.

First check technical plausibility:

- Are positions and terms correct?
- Does the action match the established formation and player roles?
- Are movements physically possible and tactically coherent?

Then apply the non-football-reader test.

A reader who understands nothing about football and is mainly here for story, characters and romance should still understand:

- what Taiga is trying to do;
- what went wrong;
- what succeeded;
- why the moment matters.

Also check:

- Is jargon explained only when necessary?
- Is there tactical detail that contributes nothing to character or story?
- Does the football scene also deliver character, relationship, conflict, comedy or progression?
- If a romance-focused reader skimmed the tactics, would they still understand the scene?

Do not substantially increase tactical density unless the tactics themselves create story stakes.

---

# 13. Exposition

Check when and why information appears.

Ask:

- Is information arriving when the reader actually needs it?
- Is something being explained twice?
- Are character biographies leaking into narration?
- Is background information embedded naturally in action or dialogue?
- Are too many names or concepts introduced simultaneously?
- Does the reader need to remember this information now, or can it wait?

---

# 14. Pacing

Check scene weight rather than word count.

Ask:

- Are important moments given enough room?
- Are transitional moments taking too much space?
- Are repeated routines being dramatized when compression would work?
- Is something being skipped that deserves an actual scene?
- Does the chapter vary density appropriately?
- Is there enough breathing room after a major beat?

Word count is not the target.

---

# 15. Cognitive Load

Check how much active attention and working memory the reader needs from scene to scene.

Ask:

- Are several dialogue-heavy or information-heavy scenes stacked without a lower-demand stretch between them?
- Is every conversation delivering new names, facts, relationship information, setup or plot at once?
- Are too many new names introduced close together, especially characters the reader does not actually need to remember?
- Does the prose clearly signal which names, facts and details matter and which are background texture?
- After a dense scene, does the reader get time to process through action, observation, atmosphere, routine, humor or silence?
- Are scene breaks providing a real cognitive reset, or does the next scene immediately resume the same information load?
- Could some dialogue be summarized or converted into action/narration without losing voice or important character beats?
- Are low-stakes conversations allowed to remain low-stakes, or has every exchange been made narratively significant?
- Does an important line have enough quieter material around it to stand out?
- Is a passage exhausting despite not being boring?

Do not add filler merely to lower the load.

Breathing room should arise naturally from the characters' schedules, actions, environment or ordinary life. Its function may simply be to let the reader inhabit the story without having to memorize or interpret something new.

---

# 16. Sentence Rhythm

Read paragraphs aloud when useful.

Watch for:

- too many fragments;
- too many very short sentences in succession;
- repeated short-short-short cadence;
- paragraphs built with the same rhythm;
- too many one-line reaction paragraphs;
- every paragraph ending on a neat punchline;
- missing longer sentences where thought or action needs flow.

Fragments are a Taiga tool.

They are not the entire narrative voice.

---

# 17. LLM-Style Pass

Look specifically for prose that feels mechanically constructed.

Watch for:

- repeated setup → explanation → conclusion structures;
- artificially neat contrasts;
- unnecessary aphorisms;
- repeated “Not X. Y.” construction;
- generic filters such as “he found himself watching”;
- abstract phrasing where a concrete observation would work better;
- narration announcing significance;
- emotional beats followed by a sentence explaining their meaning;
- excessively polished symmetry;
- dialogue conveniently articulating the exact point of the scene.

Remove these only when they genuinely feel artificial.

Do not deliberately make good prose worse in order to avoid an imagined AI detector.

---

# 18. Over-Explanation Pass

Check whether the prose trusts what it already showed.

Ask:

- If behavior already shows the emotion, can the explanatory sentence go?
- If dialogue already communicates the point, does narration repeat it?
- If Taiga already reacted physically, is the emotion then named unnecessarily?
- Is a joke explained after it lands?
- Is a relationship dynamic described immediately after being demonstrated?

---

# 19. Specificity and Abstraction

Prefer precise ordinary language.

Check vague phrases such as:

- something changed;
- he felt weird;
- there was tension;
- it felt different;
- something about him;
- for some reason.

Where useful, replace abstraction with concrete observation.

Do not replace simple language merely to sound literary.

---

# 20. Repetition

Audit repetition at multiple levels:

- repeated jokes;
- repeated Taiga reactions;
- repeated descriptions of the same trait;
- repeated information from earlier chapters;
- repeated sentence constructions;
- repeated words in close proximity;
- repeated scene functions.

Deliberate motifs are fine.

Accidental redundancy is not.

---

# 21. Chapter Opening

Check the first page as an entry point.

Ask:

- Does the chapter enter at the useful moment?
- Is there unnecessary waking-up, getting-ready or travel setup?
- Does it orient the reader enough after the previous chapter?
- Does it continue the same novel rather than reset the world?
- Is the opening hook organic rather than artificially dramatic?

---

# 22. Chapter Ending

Check whether the chapter stops at the right moment.

Ask:

- Has the chapter's main movement actually finished?
- Is the final beat about something that matters?
- Does the chapter continue after its natural ending?
- Is the ending artificially profound?
- Does it create forward momentum without requiring a cliffhanger?
- Would a final Taiga observation work better than explaining what the chapter meant?

---

# 23. Title

The title should belong to the whole chapter.

Check:

- Does it fit the chapter's overall movement rather than one isolated scene?
- Does it gain meaning from the chapter?
- Is it too clever for the actual prose?
- Does it accidentally spoil a reveal?
- Does it fit the naming style of surrounding chapters?

---

# 24. Grammar and Natural English

Perform this after content and craft decisions are stable.

Check:

- tense consistency;
- articles;
- prepositions;
- idiomatic phrasing;
- dialogue punctuation;
- commas;
- possessives;
- singular/plural agreement;
- incorrect word choice;
- Italian interference;
- awkward constructions that are grammatical but unnatural;
- typos;
- names.

Small grammar and natural-English fixes may be corrected directly at this stage.

---

# 25. Formatting

Check final Markdown and eventual publication rendering.

Audit:

- quotation-mark consistency;
- italics rules;
- text-message formatting;
- scene separators;
- escaped Markdown characters;
- formation/table/code blocks;
- heading format;
- chapter numbering;
- accidental extra whitespace;
- broken Markdown.

When unusual formatting appears, consider eventual AO3 rendering.

---

# 26. Normal-Reader Pass

Stop editing sentence by sentence.

Read the chapter as a normal reader.

Notice:

- Where do I get bored?
- Where do I get confused?
- Where do I reread because staging or information is unclear?
- Where does something feel constructed rather than lived?
- Where do I forget that I am editing because the characters carry me?
- Does the chapter feel like one coherent piece rather than a collection of revised scenes?

---

# 27. Story / Romance Reader Test

Use the likely audience as another check.

Ask:

- If someone is primarily here for Taiga/Leo and the characters, does the chapter still reward them?
- If romance barely advances, does the chapter deepen the relationships or environment that make the romance matter?
- Are non-romantic scenes still doing enough character work?
- Does Leo's presence or absence feel appropriate for this point in the story rather than artificially maximized?

The novel does not need romantic progression in every chapter.

It does need continuing human interest.

---

# 28. Must-Fix vs Taste

Before another polishing pass, classify remaining issues.

### Must fix

- continuity/error;
- clarity problem;
- character/POV inconsistency;
- grammar or natural-English error.

### Usually optional

- alternative wording;
- different rhythm that is not objectively clearer;
- minor preference;
- another possible joke;
- a sentence that could theoretically be slightly prettier.

Fix the first group.

Be suspicious of continuing to polish the second.

---

# 29. Freeze Decision

Ask:

> **Would we be comfortable publishing this chapter and forcing later chapters to live with its choices?**

If yes, freeze it.

Once frozen, reopen only for:

- typo;
- formatting issue;
- genuine continuity error.

Do not repeatedly reread a frozen chapter looking for sentences that could theoretically become slightly better.

---

# 30. Before Moving On

After freezing:

- identify any new continuity fact the next chapter must inherit;
- update a Final Bible only if a genuinely global rule emerged;
- add novel-wide issues to the Revision Ledger when appropriate;
- note any intentionally deferred issue;
- then move to the next chapter.

The purpose of this checklist is not to create another infinite editing cycle.

Its final step is always:

> **Stop editing the finished chapter and continue the novel.**
