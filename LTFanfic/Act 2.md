# Chapter Thirteen
## Unscheduled

The first snow of the semester arrived on the same afternoon everybody started leaving.

Taiga noticed because, for the first time in nearly four months, he had nowhere he was supposed to be.

That felt suspicious.

Finals had consumed the previous nine days with enough efficiency that most of them barely survived as separate memories.

Statistics happened.

Political Institutions happened.

Writing Seminar required a revision memo that Taiga wrote, hated, rewrote and eventually submitted five minutes before he could convince himself to change it again.

Foundations of Computer Science ended with a final that contained one graph problem, one recursion problem and a question about aliasing that Professor Rao had almost certainly included because she enjoyed watching half the class rediscover old trauma.

Soccer continued around all of it.

Training became shorter.

Nobody had enough energy to argue properly during film.

The reserve season paused.

The first team finished its last fixture before the break without Taiga, which bothered him less than it would have two weeks earlier and more than he wanted to admit.

Then, suddenly, Friday afternoon existed.

No class tomorrow.

No training until January.

No assignment due before next semester.

Taiga stood outside the Academic Zone with his backpack over one shoulder and watched the first flakes drift between the buildings.

They were barely snow.

Tiny white pieces that disappeared as soon as they touched the wet pavement.

People reacted anyway.

Someone near the library entrance shouted.

Two girls stopped in the middle of the path to take pictures.

A guy in a red coat held out both hands as if weather had personally delivered him a gift.

Taiga looked up.

One flake landed on his cheek and melted immediately.

“Great.”

His Oracle buzzed.

Nora.

**Nora:** IT'S SNOWING

Taiga looked around at the snow currently falling directly onto him.

**Taiga:** thanks i hadn't noticed

**Nora:** you looked like you needed confirmation

She had already left that morning.

Her mother had arrived at seven-thirty with Oliver in the back seat wearing a red sweater and an expression of profound personal betrayal.

Nora had sent photographic evidence.

Taiga had saved one picture because the dog looked ridiculous.

That meant nothing.

Another message appeared.

**Nora:** train still tomorrow?

**Taiga:** 9:12

**Nora:** tell the camp people i said hi

Taiga stopped walking.

He had never introduced Nora to anyone from Camp Buddy.

She knew enough about them now that apparently greetings could travel by proxy.

Weird.

**Taiga:** no

**Nora:** perfect thank you

Taiga locked the Oracle.

He crossed Central Park toward the Residential Zone.

Campus had already changed.

Suitcases moved everywhere.

Parents waited at curbs.

Cars idled outside residence halls.

Students hugged people they had known for one semester with the emotional intensity of soldiers leaving for war despite most of them returning in three weeks.

Taiga had mocked this yesterday.

Then Caleb had left.

Not dramatically.

Maya arrived at ten with two reusable shopping bags, one backpack and an argument already in progress about whether Caleb needed three pairs of shoes for the break.

Caleb said yes.

Maya said no.

Taiga stayed out of it.

Five minutes before leaving, Caleb had checked the window latch, unplugged the extra power strip, confirmed the mini-fridge temperature and stood beside the door looking around the room.

Taiga had been sitting at his desk.

“Did you lose something?”

“No.”

“Then why are you staring?”

“I am checking whether I forgot anything.”

“You've checked four times.”

“Yes.”

Maya called from the hallway, “Five!”

Caleb ignored her.

Then he looked at Taiga.

“I will be back January sixth.”

“I know.”

“The refrigerator is on setting three.”

“I know.”

“Do not turn it to four.”

“Why would I?”

“The back-left section freezes liquids.”

“I know.”

Caleb paused.

Taiga waited.

Then Caleb said, “Have a good break.”

Something about the sentence had felt strangely formal.

Taiga answered anyway.

“You too.”

Maya leaned around the doorframe.

“Can I have him now?”

“He's your problem.”

Caleb picked up his suitcase.

Maya smiled.

“Bye, Taiga.”

“Bye.”

They left.

That was it.

No analysis of Taiga's face.

No observation about what he was secretly feeling.

Just a door closing and half the room becoming noticeably more empty.

Taiga had disliked that for approximately ten minutes.

Then he'd enjoyed having the room to himself.

Both things could apparently be true.

Annoying.

Now snow collected along the grass as he crossed toward the Residential Zone.

His train to Camp Buddy left tomorrow morning.

Yoshinori had offered to have someone pick him up from the station.

Yoichi had responded to this information with:

**Yoichi:** walk

Keitaro had replied:

**Keitaro:** It's fourteen kilometers, Yoichi.

**Yoichi:** good

Taiga had not answered.

He was looking forward to going back.

That fact no longer required an argument.

He reached room 317 at three-sixteen.

The silence hit immediately.

No keyboard from Caleb's side.

No low music through his headphones.

No half-finished glass of water placed exactly six centimeters from the edge of the desk because apparently that was where glasses lived.

Taiga dropped his bag.

He should pack.

Instead he sat on the bed.

Then lay backward.

The ceiling looked exactly like it had yesterday.

For some reason that was disappointing.

He closed his eyes.

Nothing due.

Nothing scheduled.

Nothing to fix.

His brain lasted approximately forty seconds before producing:

*Sometimes I forget there was a version of Olympus where you weren't here yet.*

Taiga opened his eyes.

“No.”

The empty room did not respond.

Good.

He had managed not to think about that sentence for most of finals.

Mostly because finals had provided several hundred more urgent things to think about.

Unfortunately, the sentence had apparently survived.

It had returned at random.

Once while Leo was arguing with Enzo about whether a burrito counted as structurally compromised if one end opened.

Once when Leo had borrowed Taiga's pen during film, used it for forty minutes and then forgotten it was not his.

Once when Taiga had walked into Apollo and heard Leo laughing from upstairs before seeing him.

That one had been particularly stupid.

Nothing had happened.

Taiga had simply recognized the laugh.

People recognized sounds.

Normal.

The sentence meant nothing.

Leo had explicitly said so.

Taiga rolled onto his side.

His Oracle buzzed on the desk.

He ignored it.

Buzz.

Again.

“Fuck.”

He reached over.

Leo.

Of course.

**Leo:** ARE YOU STILL HERE

Taiga stared at the screen.

Then at the ceiling.

The timing was offensive.

**Taiga:** where else would i be

Three dots appeared immediately.

**Leo:** I DON'T KNOW EVERYBODY ABANDONED CAMPUS AT ONCE

**Leo:** enzo left at noon, aaron left after lunch, gav disappeared without saying goodbye like a divorced father, mia has been sending me airport pictures for two hours and scott apparently left YESTERDAY without informing civilization

Taiga smiled despite himself.

**Taiga:** sounds like they planned their travel

**Leo:** betrayal can be planned

Then:

**Leo:** snow

Taiga looked out the window.

White had started gathering on the courtyard grass.

**Taiga:** yes that is what happens in winter

**Leo:** CENTRAL PARK HAS THE RINK OPEN

Taiga sat up slightly.

There was a seasonal rink in the city park.

He knew because Nora had mentioned it twice and because an advertisement had occupied half the Central Station wall since November.

He had not considered going.

**Taiga:** congratulations to central park

**Leo:** come with me

Taiga stopped.

Simple sentence.

Three words.

His chest did something small and irritating.

No.

Absolutely not.

Leo sent another message before Taiga answered.

**Leo:** PLEASE i have spent the last forty minutes trying to convince people and apparently everyone suddenly has families

There.

Better.

Not specific.

Taiga was simply the remaining available human.

That made sense.

**Taiga:** you also have a family

**Leo:** yes and they have been informed i am arriving tomorrow

**Leo:** tonight i am exercising my constitutional right to make bad decisions on ice

Taiga laughed.

Political Institutions had damaged everyone.

**Taiga:** i have to pack

**Leo:** how long does that take

Taiga looked at the open suitcase under his bed.

Twenty minutes.

Maybe thirty if he had to decide what not to bring.

**Taiga:** hours

**Leo:** liar

Then:

**Leo:** 4:10 central station

**Leo:** if you don't come i will respect your decision and complain about it forever

Taiga stared.

He could say no.

There was no reason not to.

He had gone out enough this semester.

He had a train tomorrow.

He should pack.

He should probably call Yoshinori.

He should—

His eyes went to the window.

Snow.

Actual snow now.

Not much.

Enough.

Taiga typed:

**Taiga:** 4:15

Leo reacted with six sun emojis.

Taiga immediately regretted everything.

At four-sixteen, Leo was standing beneath the Central Station clock wearing a yellow scarf Taiga had never seen before.

It was terrible.

Not objectively.

Probably.

It was just very yellow.

Leo spotted him across the concourse and lifted both arms.

“You came!”

Taiga stopped in front of him.

“You gave me a time and place.”

“Yeah, but you say no to things with incredible commitment. There was still suspense.”

“I say yes sometimes.”

Leo started walking toward the platform.

“You say maybe, then appear. Different system.”

“Nora says that too.”

“See? Independent peer review.”

Leo caught him.

“You hate it that much?”

“I was trying to decide whether it qualifies as protective equipment.”

“My sister gave it to me two years ago because she said yellow makes me look less boring.”

Taiga looked at the knitted fabric.

“She chose that color on purpose?”

“She calls it gold. It is not gold. Gold does not make drivers slow down because they think a warning sign escaped.”

Taiga laughed.

“How old is she?”

Leo's eyes lit up.

“See? Now we're exchanging personal information.”

“The scarf, idiot.”

“Oh. Two years.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“You had your chance.”

Taiga shook his head and kept walking.

Taiga smiled.

They boarded the train.

The carriage was almost empty.

That felt wrong too.

Normally Central line trains at four-thirty contained enough students to make standing a negotiation.

Today there were twelve people in the entire car.

Leo sat by the window.

Taiga took the seat opposite.

Snow streaked past the glass.

Leo checked his Oracle.

“My mother has asked whether my train tomorrow is still running three times.”

“Is it?”

“Yes.”

“Then why three times?”

“Because weather exists.”

“That doesn't answer anything.”

“It answers mothers.”

Taiga looked at him.

Leo smiled and typed something.

“You going straight to Camp Buddy tomorrow?”

“Yeah.”

“How long?”

“Most of the break.”

“That's actually really cool. I still can't picture it properly.”

“What?”

“Camp Buddy. I know what a summer camp looks like in theory, but every time you talk about yours it sounds like a small country run by six people and one angry dog.”

Taiga laughed.

“That's not completely wrong.”

“See? I want evidence.”

“You've seen pictures.”

“I've seen Keitaro pictures. Those are different. Keitaro takes pictures like he's documenting the emotional decline of a chair.”

Taiga stared.

Then laughed hard enough that the woman two seats away looked over.

Leo pointed.

“Exactly! Yoichi was right.”

“Yoichi said the same thing.”

“I know. He has vision.”

“Don't tell him that.”

“Never.”

The train left the university district.

For once Leo did not know anyone in the carriage.

This appeared to create excess conversational capacity.

He used it.

He told Taiga about getting home tomorrow.

About apparently having promised three different people that he would do three different things during the break before realizing two of them happened on the same day.

About his mother informing him that university had not improved his calendar management.

About wanting to sleep for twelve hours and also already having plans the morning after he arrived.

Taiga listened.

Then said:

“You know you can say no.”

Leo looked genuinely surprised.

“I can.”

“Yes.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

Leo pointed at him.

“Okay, don't use the Caleb voice on me.”

“That is my voice.”

“No, there's a specific version where you become aggressively reasonable and it's terrible.”

Taiga smiled.

“You signed up for three things on the same day.”

“Two things. The third one is flexible.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means nobody gave me a time.”

“That is not what flexible means.”

“It is now.”

The train slowed.

Leo looked outside.

Then back at Taiga.

“Also, saying yes usually works out.”

“Your evidence for that is terrible.”

“My evidence is that I'm here.”

Taiga opened his mouth.

Stopped.

Leo apparently meant the university.

Obviously.

Or the train.

Either.

It did not matter.

Taiga looked out the window.

Snow had started sticking to rooftops.

Leo said nothing for approximately five seconds.

Then:

“Your ears are red.”

Taiga turned.

“What?”

Leo grinned.

“Cold?”

“Yes.”

They were inside a heated train.

Leo did not mention that.

Points in his favor.

Central Park had been converted into something aggressively seasonal.

Lights wrapped every tree along the main path.

Temporary wooden stalls formed two rows beside the lawn.

A giant illuminated arch announced **OLYMPUS WINTER WEEK** as if winter required branding.

Music played from somewhere near the fountain.

The fountain itself had been turned off and covered in lights.

The rink occupied the broad plaza beyond it.

Taiga stopped at the entrance.

There were more people than expected.

Families.

Students.

Children moving at dangerous speeds.

Couples.

Too many couples.

That observation arrived without permission.

Taiga immediately deleted it from consideration.

Leo inhaled dramatically beside him.

“Oh, this is perfect.”

“It is crowded.”

“That's what perfect looks like sometimes.”

“That sentence is disgusting.”

Leo laughed and pulled him toward the rental booth.

Not physically.

Mostly.

He did catch Taiga's sleeve for half a second when Taiga started toward the wrong line.

Taiga noticed the fingers.

That was stupid.

The fingers released.

Problem solved.

They rented skates.

Taiga sat on a bench and looked at his pair.

Leo dropped beside him.

“You've done this before, right?”

“No.”

Leo stopped tying one lace.

“Never?”

“No.”

“You worked at a camp.”

“In summer.”

“Oh.”

Taiga looked at him.

“What?”

“I don't know why my brain just decided summer camps contain ice.”

“Because your brain is damaged.”

“Probably.”

“I've done it like... four times.”

“That's your qualification?”

“Four more than you.”

“Terrifying.”

“Don't worry. Soccer balance transfers.”

“Does it?”

“No idea.”

Taiga stared.

Leo smiled brightly.

“Come on.”

The first thirty seconds were humiliating.

Not because Taiga fell.

He did not fall.

That would have provided a clear event.

Instead he stepped onto the ice and immediately discovered that every movement his body normally used to stop itself from sliding was now wrong.

His ankles felt unstable.

The rink barrier became the most trustworthy object in Olympus City.

Leo pushed away beside him with much more confidence than four previous skating experiences justified.

“Okay. Bend your knees a little.”

“I know.”

“You are standing like somebody put a weapon against your spine.”

“I said I know.”

“Push sideways, not backward.”

Taiga tried.

Moved approximately one meter.

Stopped badly.

Leo clapped once.

“Beautiful.”

“Fuck off.”

“No, seriously. You moved.”

“I walk every day.”

“Different surface.”

Taiga pushed again.

Better.

A child passed him at roughly highway speed.

Taiga looked after her.

“That child is six.”

“She's probably been skating since birth.”

“That is not possible.”

“You don't know her life.”

Leo skated backward in front of him.

This was irritating.

“Stop doing that.”

“Why?”

“Because if you fall while teaching me, I'm leaving you here.”

“I am not going to fall.”

He nearly fell immediately.

Taiga laughed.

Leo recovered with both arms spread.

“Controlled demonstration.”

“Of failure?”

“Of recovery.”

They completed one lap in nine minutes.

Taiga's second lap took six.

By the third, his body had started understanding enough to become dangerous.

He could move.

Stopping remained theoretical.

Leo had become overconfident again.

He skated ahead, turned, and started moving backward.

“You see? I told you soccer transfers. Your hips already understand not to lock up, which is the hard part. Most people do this—”

He demonstrated something exaggerated and ugly.

Taiga laughed.

“That's not skating.”

“Exactly. That's panic with blades.”

“You looked like that five minutes ago.”

“Lies.”

A teenager cut across between them.

Leo looked sideways.

Taiga saw the collision before Leo did.

“Leo.”

Leo turned back.

Too late.

He twisted to avoid the teenager.

His skate caught awkwardly.

One arm windmilled.

Taiga moved on instinct.

He grabbed Leo around the waist.

Momentum handled the rest badly.

Leo hit the barrier with his back.

Not hard.

Taiga hit Leo.

Much harder than he intended.

For one second, everything stopped.

Taiga's left hand was flattened against the barrier beside Leo's shoulder.

His right arm was still around Leo's waist.

Leo had one hand gripping Taiga's upper arm.

The other was pressed against Taiga's chest.

They were close.

Very close.

Close enough that Taiga could see the tiny darker ring around Leo's irises.

Close enough to see three snowflakes melting in his hair.

Close enough that Leo's breath touched the lower half of his face before turning white between them.

Taiga's brain, which had successfully processed moving defenders, academic deadlines, statistics exams and one entire semester of university, stopped.

Leo stared at him.

His mouth opened.

Nothing came out.

That was alarming by itself.

Leo always had something to say.

Taiga became aware of his arm.

Around Leo.

Specifically around Leo's waist.

He released him so quickly he nearly lost his own balance.

Leo caught his sleeve.

“Whoa—”

“I'm fine.”

“I know, I just—”

Leo stopped.

His face was red.

Not slightly.

Red from cheekbones to ears.

Taiga stared.

“You're red.”

Excellent.

Brilliant contribution.

Leo blinked.

Then laughed too loudly.

“It's freezing!”

Taiga looked at him.

The rink lights reflected off the ice.

Snow landed on Leo's scarf.

“It wasn't freezing ten seconds ago?”

Leo's eyes widened.

Taiga realized what he'd said.

Meaning he had noticed Leo's face before.

Fuck.

Heat climbed into Taiga's own ears immediately.

Leo noticed.

Of course he did.

The corner of his mouth started climbing.

Taiga pointed at him.

“Wipe that off.”

“This is my normal face.”

“No, it isn't.”

“I smile all the time.”

“Not like that.”

They both froze again.

Because apparently Taiga had started keeping records.

Leo's grin disappeared for half a second.

Then returned smaller.

“Okay,” he said.

One word.

Leo.

One word.

“Okay, wow, you know what? We should keep moving before my dignity freezes to the barrier, because I nearly murdered a teenager and then got body-checked by you in front of approximately forty witnesses, so I think I've reached my humiliation quota.”

There.

Normal Leo.

“You were the one moving backward.”

“I was educating.”

“You were showing off.”

“Those can overlap.”

Leo pushed away from the wall.

Taiga followed.

His legs worked.

Mostly.

They did not discuss the collision.

This was correct.

Then another.

Taiga got better.

Leo stopped skating backward.

Probably unrelated.

Also unrelated.

Showers existed.

None.

“You good?”

“Because you just attacked a civilian.”

Good.

No.

Wrong problem.

Too much.
