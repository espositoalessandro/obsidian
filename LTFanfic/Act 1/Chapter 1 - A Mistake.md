The first thing Taiga Akatora learned about Olympus University was that apparently nobody there understood the concept of shutting the hell up.

There were whistles somewhere beyond the residence halls. Music leaked from an open second-floor window, not loudly enough to be called a party and somehow loudly enough to make the bass follow him across the courtyard. Two students in matching navy orientation shirts stood beside the main path shouting directions to freshmen despite the fact that there were signs approximately every twelve feet. A golf cart beeped at a family who had stopped in the middle of the road to redistribute luggage. Somebody nearby was laughing hard enough to cough.

And above all of it, bells were ringing from the stone tower at the center of campus. Taiga stopped on the pavement and stared.

Olympus University rose around him in pale stone and dark glass, old buildings tangled up with new ones as if somebody had started with a postcard of a prestigious private college and then kept adding things whenever a donor became bored. Ivy climbed the oldest halls. Banners hung from the lampposts, navy and gold, each stamped with the mountain-and-laurel emblem he had already seen on emails, envelopes, financial-aid documents, the housing portal, his student card and one extremely expensive sweatshirt he had absolutely not bought.

**WELCOME, CLASS OF—**

He looked away before finishing it.

“Jesus Christ.”

A mother passing with a floor lamp glanced at him. Taiga pretended he hadn't spoken. He adjusted the duffel strap digging into his shoulder and checked the campus map on the phone again. The Residential Zone marker was almost on top of his location dot. He looked up. His residence hall was thirty feet away.

“Great.”

He shoved the phone back into his pocket and pulled his suitcase across the courtyard. The suitcase wheels rattled over every seam in the pavement. The heat stuck his shirt to the middle of his back. Somebody in front of him had decided that walking three abreast at half normal speed was the correct way to move through a crowded entrance.

By the time he reached the doors, he had been on campus for less than ten minutes and already wanted everybody to go home. A group of freshmen blocked half the entrance while taking a photograph. One of them stepped backward without looking and nearly walked straight into him.

“Watch it.”

The guy spun around. “Oh—shit, sorry. I didn't see you.” Taiga's answer arrived automatically. *Clearly.* It was already at the back of his teeth. He stopped it there. One second.

Camp Buddy had taught him a ridiculous number of things he had never expected to use again. How to tie a canoe to a dock properly. How to tell poison ivy from Virginia creeper. How to cook eggs for forty people without making half of them rubber. How to tell when Hiro was about to do something stupid from the particular expression he made immediately before doing it.

It had also, through a much more painful process, taught Taiga that not every irritation needed to become everybody else's problem. The guy was still waiting. Taiga exhaled through his nose.

“Whatever. Just move.”

“Yeah. Sorry again.”

The group shifted enough to let him pass. Taiga continued inside. He hated progress.

His first real Political Institutions lecture almost ruined his mood. Callback was harder. Fewer players meant fewer places to hide a bad touch. Taiga arrived early enough to watch an assistant set out mannequins and flat markers along the left side of the pitch. Mercer split the group by position, then stopped in front of Taiga and three other wide players.

“You four are wingers today.”

One of them nodded immediately. Taiga did too, half a second later. Mercer noticed.

“Akatora. What does that mean to you?”

“Wide attacker.”

“Good start. It does not mean ‘stand next to the touchline and wait for the ball.’ Your job changes depending on what the fullback does.”

He pointed behind Taiga, where another player stood deeper on the same side.

“That is the fullback. Defender first. When we have the ball, he can come forward outside you. That run is an overlap.”

The fullback jogged past the mannequin to demonstrate. Mercer moved Taiga two steps inward.

“If he goes outside, you usually do not occupy the same strip of grass. Come inside. Make the defender choose. Follow you and the overlap is free. Stay with the overlap and you can receive inside.”

Taiga looked at the shape. That made sense. Mercer pointed at the channel between the wide defender and the nearest center-back.

“This space is the half-space. Coaches call it that because apparently ‘the annoying bit between wide and central’ was too long.”

A couple of players laughed. Taiga did too.

“Do I always move inside?”

“No. Football would be much easier if every answer was always.” Mercer stepped back. “Read the defender. Read your fullback. Read where the ball is. The diagram gives you options, not permission to stop thinking.”

That part Taiga liked less. They started. First repetition: the fullback went. Taiga stayed wide. Whistle.

“Akatora.”

“Yeah. Inside.”

“Then go there before I have to tell you.”

Reset. Second repetition, Taiga moved too early and dragged himself into the central midfielder's lane. Mercer stopped them again.

“Different problem. Same cause.”

Taiga frowned.

“I moved.”

“You moved because you remembered a rule. I need you to move because you saw something.”

Heat climbed his neck. Third repetition. He checked over his shoulder first. Fullback starting high. Defender watching him. Central midfielder already occupying the inside lane. Taiga held width for another second. Then the midfielder dropped, the lane opened, and Taiga stepped into it. Pass inside. One touch. Fullback around him. Return ball outside. Clean. Mercer said, “Better,” and was already resetting the drill. Good. They ran it from the other side and everything became backwards.

Taiga wanted to murder geometry. Later came pressing. That word he knew. The details were worse. Mercer put a ball at the center-back's feet.

“You do not sprint at every person who receives it. Pressing is coordinated pressure. If Akatora goes and the rest of you stay home, Akatora is not pressing. He is jogging angrily by himself.”

Someone behind Taiga laughed. Taiga turned. Leo Mendez was grinning openly. Taiga glared at him. Leo pressed his lips together with exaggerated effort. “Sorry. Mercer just described your entire personality as a tactical error.”

“Keep smiling and I’ll make it a physical one.”

“That sounded affectionate.”

Mercer blew the whistle before Taiga could answer.

“Again. Ball goes to their right-back. That is your trigger, Akatora. Curve the run so you close the line outside and show him toward our midfield.”

“Why inside?”

“Because Brennan and Romero are waiting there. We want the pass he thinks is safe.”

Taiga looked toward the two central players Mercer had indicated. Gav Brennan gave him a small nod. Isaac Romero tapped two fingers against his temple. Read it. Fine. They reset.

By the sixth repetition Taiga could feel the structure rather than recite it. Then the scrimmage began and the structure immediately became less polite. Players moved before he expected them to. Passing lanes existed for half a second. A correct position became wrong when somebody else made a different decision. He lost the ball six times. Won it five. Created one assist after beating the right-back and cutting the ball toward Jordan Price, the striker, who finished first time.

Price pointed at Taiga while jogging back.

“Do that again.”

Taiga almost told him to fuck off. Then realized it was probably praise. He missed an easy shot ten minutes later badly enough that he intended to remember it at three in the morning for the next decade. Under the stadium lights, the coaches gathered everybody at midfield. Final selections would be emailed the following afternoon. Thank you for coming. That was it. Taiga picked up his bag with his stomach tied into a knot.

Waiting was worse than failure. Failure, at least, was information.
