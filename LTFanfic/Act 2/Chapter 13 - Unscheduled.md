The first snow of the semester arrived on the same afternoon everybody started leaving. Taiga noticed because, for the first time in nearly four months, he had nowhere he was supposed to be, which felt suspicious.

Finals had consumed the previous nine days with enough efficiency that most of them barely survived as separate memories. Calculus happened. Political Institutions happened. General Chemistry required a lab write-up that Taiga wrote, hated, rewrote and eventually submitted five minutes before he could convince himself to change it again. Foundations of Computer Science ended with a final that contained one Quicksort problem, one recursion problem and a question about aliasing that Professor Rao had almost certainly included because she enjoyed watching half the class rediscover old trauma.

Soccer continued around all of it, although training became shorter and nobody had enough energy to argue properly during film. The reserve season paused. The first team finished its last fixture before the break without Taiga, which bothered him less than it would have two weeks earlier and more than he wanted to admit.

Then, suddenly, Friday afternoon existed. No class tomorrow, no training until January, no assignment due before next semester.

Taiga stood outside the Academic Zone with his backpack over one shoulder and watched the first flakes drift between the buildings. They were barely snow, tiny white pieces that disappeared as soon as they touched the wet pavement, but people reacted anyway. Someone near the library entrance shouted. Two girls stopped in the middle of the path to take pictures, while a guy in a red coat held out both hands as if weather had personally delivered him a gift.

Taiga looked up. One flake landed on his cheek and melted immediately.

“Great.”

His phone buzzed. Nora.

**Nora:** IT'S SNOWING

Taiga looked around at the snow currently falling directly onto him.

**Taiga:** thanks i hadn't noticed

**Nora:** you looked like you needed confirmation

She had already left that morning. Her mother had arrived at seven-thirty with Oliver in the back seat wearing a red sweater and an expression of profound personal betrayal, and Nora had sent photographic evidence. Taiga had saved one because the dog looked ridiculous.

Another message appeared.

**Nora:** train still tomorrow?

**Taiga:** 9:12

**Nora:** tell the camp people i said hi

Taiga slowed. He had never introduced Nora to anyone from Camp Buddy, but she knew enough about them now that apparently greetings could travel by proxy. Weird.

**Taiga:** no

**Nora:** perfect thank you

He locked the phone and crossed Central Park toward the Residential Zone. Campus had already changed; suitcases moved everywhere, parents waited at curbs, cars idled outside residence halls. Students hugged people they had known for one semester with the emotional intensity of soldiers leaving for war despite most of them returning in three weeks.

Taiga had mocked this yesterday. Then Caleb had left.

Not dramatically. Maya arrived at ten with two reusable shopping bags, one backpack and an argument already in progress about whether Caleb needed three pairs of shoes for the break. Caleb said yes, Maya said no, and Taiga stayed out of it.

Five minutes before leaving, Caleb had checked the window latch, unplugged the extra power strip, confirmed the mini-fridge temperature and stood beside the door looking around the room while Taiga sat at his desk.

“Did you lose something?”

“No.”

“Then why are you staring?”

“I am checking whether I forgot anything.”

“You've checked four times.”

“Yes.”

Maya called from the hallway, “Five!”

Caleb ignored her, then looked at Taiga. “I will be back January sixth.”

“I know.”

A small pause followed before Caleb said, “Have a good break.”

Something about the sentence felt strangely formal.

“You too.”

Maya leaned around the doorframe. “Can I have him now?”

“He's your problem.”

Caleb picked up his suitcase, and Maya smiled at Taiga. “Bye.”

“Bye.”

The door closed behind them, leaving half the room immediately emptier. Taiga disliked that for approximately ten minutes. Then he enjoyed having the room to himself.

Now snow collected along the grass as he crossed toward Residence. His train to Camp Buddy left tomorrow morning. Yoshinori had offered to have someone pick him up from the station; Yoichi had responded to this information with a single message.

**Yoichi:** walk

Keitaro had replied almost immediately.

**Keitaro:** It's fourteen kilometers, Yoichi.

**Yoichi:** good

Taiga had not answered. He was looking forward to going back, and that fact no longer required an argument.

He reached room 317 at three-sixteen. The silence hit immediately: no keyboard from Caleb's side, no low music through his headphones, no half-finished glass of water placed exactly six centimeters from the edge of the desk because apparently that was where glasses lived.

Taiga dropped his bag. He should pack, but instead he sat on the bed and lay backward. The ceiling looked exactly like it had yesterday, which for some reason was disappointing.

Nothing due. Nothing scheduled. Nothing to fix.

His brain lasted approximately forty seconds before producing a sentence Leo had said weeks ago.

*Sometimes I forget there was a version of Olympus where you weren't here yet.*

Taiga opened his eyes.

“No.”

The empty room did not respond. Good.

He had managed not to think about that sentence for most of finals, but it had still turned up occasionally: once while Leo was arguing with Enzo about whether a burrito counted as structurally compromised if one end opened, once when Leo borrowed Taiga's pen during film and forgot for forty minutes that it was not his, and once when Taiga walked into Apollo and heard Leo laughing upstairs before he saw him.

His phone buzzed on the desk. Taiga ignored it until it buzzed again.

“Fuck.”

He reached over. Leo, of course.

**Leo:** ARE YOU STILL HERE

Taiga stared at the screen, then at the ceiling. The timing was offensive.

**Taiga:** where else would i be

Three dots appeared immediately.

**Leo:** I DON'T KNOW EVERYBODY ABANDONED CAMPUS AT ONCE

**Leo:** enzo left at noon, aaron left after lunch, gav disappeared without saying goodbye like a divorced father, mia has been sending me airport pictures for two hours and scott apparently left YESTERDAY without informing civilization

Taiga smiled.

**Taiga:** sounds like they planned their travel

**Leo:** betrayal can be planned

A second later:

**Leo:** anyways, there's snow

Taiga looked out the window. White had started gathering on the courtyard grass.

**Taiga:** yes that is what happens in winter

**Leo:** central park has the rink open

There was a seasonal rink in the city park. Taiga knew because Nora had mentioned it twice and because an advertisement had occupied half the Central Station wall since November, although he had never seriously considered going.

**Taiga:** congratulations to central park

**Leo:** come with me?

Taiga read that twice. Before he answered, another message appeared.

**Leo:** PLEASE i have spent the last forty minutes trying to convince people and apparently everyone suddenly has families

**Taiga:** you also have a family

**Leo:** yes and they have been informed i am arriving tomorrow

**Leo:** tonight i am exercising my constitutional right to make bad decisions on ice

**Taiga:** i have to pack

**Taiga:** hours

**Leo:** liar

Then:

**Leo:** 4:10 central station

**Taiga:** 4:15

**Leo:** ☀️☀️☀️☀️☀️☀️

At four-sixteen, Leo was standing beneath the Central Station clock wearing a yellow scarf Taiga had never seen before. It was terrible. Not because there was anything objectively wrong with it; it was simply very, very yellow.

Leo spotted him across the concourse and lifted both arms. “You came!”

“You gave me a time and place.”

“Yeah, but you say no to things with incredible commitment. There was still suspense.” Leo started toward the platform before Taiga could answer.

“I say yes sometimes.”

“You say maybe, then appear. Different system.”

“Nora says that too.”

“See? Independent peer review.”

Taiga looked at the scarf again, and Leo caught him.

“You hate it that much?”

“I was trying to decide whether it qualifies as protective equipment.”

Leo grabbed one end and held it out. “My sister gave it to me two years ago because she said yellow makes me look less boring.”

“She chose that color on purpose?”

“She calls it gold. It is not gold. Gold does not make drivers slow down because they think a warning sign escaped.”

Taiga laughed. “How old is she?”  

Leo’s eyes lit up. “See? Now we’re exchanging personal information.”  

“Your sister, idiot.”  

“The scarf’s two years old.”  

Taiga stared at him.  

Leo smiled. “That’s all you’re getting.”

Taiga shook his head and followed him onto the train.

The carriage was almost empty. Normally Central line trains at four-thirty contained enough students to make standing a negotiation, but today there were twelve people in the entire car. Leo sat by the window while Taiga took the seat opposite, and snow streaked past the glass as Leo checked his phone.

“My mother has asked whether my flight tomorrow is still on three times.”

“Is it?”

“Yes.”

“Then why three times?”

“Because weather exists.”

“That doesn't answer anything.”

“It answers mothers.”

Leo smiled and typed something. After a moment he looked up again. “You going straight to Camp Buddy tomorrow?”

“Yeah.”

“How long?”

“A week.”

“That's actually really cool. I still can't picture it properly.”

“What?”

“Camp Buddy. I know what a summer camp looks like in theory, but every time you talk about yours it sounds like a small country run by four people and one angry dog.”

Taiga laughed. “That's not completely wrong.”

“See? I want evidence.”

“You've seen pictures.”

“I've seen Keitaro pictures. Those are different. Keitaro takes pictures like he's documenting the emotional decline of a chair.”

Taiga stared at him for a second before laughing hard enough that the woman two seats away looked over.

“Yoichi said the same thing.”

“He has vision.”

“Don't tell him that.”

“Never.”

The train left the university district. For once Leo did not know anyone in the carriage, which appeared to create excess conversational capacity. He used it to tell Taiga about getting home tomorrow, about apparently promising three different people three different things during the break before realizing two happened on the same day, about his mother informing him that university had not improved his calendar management, and about wanting to sleep for twelve hours while also having plans the morning after he arrived.

Taiga let him go for a while before saying, “You know you can say no.”

Leo looked genuinely surprised. “I can.”

“Yes.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

Leo pointed at him. “Okay, don't use the Caleb voice on me.”

“That is my voice.”

“No, there's a specific version where you become aggressively reasonable and it's terrible.”

Taiga snorted, “You signed up for three things on the same day.”

“Two things. The third one is flexible.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means nobody gave me a time.”

“That is not what flexible means.”

“It is now.”

The train slowed. Leo glanced outside before looking back at him. “Also, saying yes usually works out.”

“Your evidence for that is terrible.”

“My evidence is that I'm here.”

Taiga shook his head, smiling. He looked through the window. Snow had started sticking to the rooftops.

---

Central Park had been converted into something aggressively seasonal. Lights wrapped every tree along the main path, temporary wooden stalls formed two rows beside the lawn, and a giant illuminated arch announced **WINTER WEEK** as if winter required branding. Music played from somewhere near the fountain, which had itself been turned off and covered in lights, while the rink occupied the broad plaza beyond it.

There were more people than Taiga expected: families, students, children moving at dangerous speeds, couples trying to skate while holding hands and mostly getting in everybody else's way.

Leo inhaled dramatically beside him. “Oh, this is perfect.”

“It is crowded.”

“That's what perfect looks like sometimes.”

“That sentence is disgusting.”

Leo laughed and headed toward the rental booth.

They rented skates and found a bench. Taiga picked up his pair with immediate suspicion while Leo dropped beside him and started tying one lace.

“You've done this before, right?”

“No.”

Leo stopped. “Never?”

“No.”

“You were at a camp.”

“In summer.”

“Fair.”

Taiga looked at him. “What?”

“I don't know why my brain just decided summer camps contain ice.”

“Because your brain is damaged.”

“Probably.” Leo tightened his skate. “Well, I've done it like... four times.”

“That's your qualification?”

“Four more than you.”

“Terrifying.”

“Don't worry. Soccer balance transfers.”

“Does it?”

“No idea.”

Taiga stared at him until Leo smiled brightly and stood.

“Come on!”

The first thirty seconds were humiliating, although not because Taiga fell. Falling would at least have provided a clear event. Instead he stepped onto the ice and immediately discovered that every movement his body normally used to stop itself from sliding was now wrong. His ankles felt unstable, and the rink barrier became the most trustworthy object in Olympus City.

Leo pushed away beside him with much more confidence than four previous skating experiences justified.

“Okay. Bend your knees a little.”

Taiga tried

“You are standing like somebody put a weapon against your spine.”

“I'm trying not to fall.”

“Push sideways, not backward.”

Taiga tried again. He moved approximately one meter and stopped badly.

Leo clapped once. “Beautiful.”

“Fuck off.”

“No, seriously. You moved.”

“I walk every day.”

“Different surface.”

The next push was better. A child passed him at roughly highway speed, and Taiga watched her disappear around the curve with a depressed face.

“That child is six.”

“She's probably been skating since birth.”

“That is not possible.”

“You don't know her life.”

Leo moved backward in front of him.

“Stop doing that.”

“Why?”

“Because if you fall while teaching me, I'm leaving you here.”

“I am not going to fall.”

He nearly fell immediately.

Taiga laughed while Leo recovered with both arms spread.

“Controlled demonstration,” Leo said.

“Of failure?”

“Of recovery.”

They completed the first lap in nine minutes and the second in six. By the third, Taiga's body had started understanding enough to become dangerous. He could move now; stopping remained theoretical.

Leo, meanwhile, had become overconfident again. He skated ahead, turned, and started moving backward.

“You see? I told you soccer transfers. Your hips already understand not to lock up, which is the hard part. Most people do this—”

He demonstrated something exaggerated and ugly.

Taiga laughed. “That's not skating.”

“Exactly. That's panic with blades.”

“You looked like that five minutes ago.”

“Lies.”

A teenager cut across between them. Leo looked sideways, and Taiga saw the collision before he did.

“Watch out!”

Leo turned back too late. He twisted to avoid the teenager, but his skate caught awkwardly and one arm windmilled.

Taiga moved on instinct and grabbed him around the waist. Leo had already lost his balance when Taiga reached him, which ended up pulling both of them on the ground.

Leo fell on the ice on his back. Taiga was on top of him. One of his hands left his waist just in time to stop him from hitting Leo with his own head. 
Leo's hands were clinging on Taiga's jacket, pulling him towards him.
They were close enough that Taiga could see the darker ring around Leo's irises and three snowflakes melting in his hair, their breath turning white between them.

Leo stared at him. His mouth opened, but nothing came out.

He could feel his own ears starting to acquire more heat than necessary.
That, more than anything, made Taiga move. 

“Well, that was embarrassing.”

Taiga didn't answer immediately. He glanced at Leo and saw that his cheeks were a bit more colored than the usual.

"...Yeah."

They stayed sit on the ice. Neither of them moved for another second. Then Leo looked past him at the teenager already halfway around the rink.

“Okay. I was nearly murdered by a minor.”

Taiga laughed before he could stop himself.

Leo pushed himself up. “I think I've reached my humiliation quota.”

“You were the one moving backward.”

“I was educating.”

“You were showing off.”

“Teaching requires demonstration.”

They completed another lap, and then another. Taiga got better. Leo stopped skating backward, although Taiga did not mention it.

At one point Taiga misjudged a turn and nearly took out a man wearing a blue hat.

“Sorry.”

The man waved him off.

Leo looked over. “You just attacked a civilian.”

“Keep skating.”

“Yes, coach.”

They left the rink twenty minutes later.

Leo wanted hot chocolate. Taiga wanted coffee. The nearest stall sold both and charged enough to qualify as organized theft, but Taiga paid anyway.

They carried the drinks toward a row of benches under strings of lights. Snow had thickened—not enough to become a storm, but enough that the park paths were turning white at the edges. Leo sat down, took a drink immediately and burned his mouth.

“Fuck.”

“You watched them pour it.”

“I believed in thermodynamics.”

“You waited six seconds.”

“I was optimistic.”

“That's your problem.”

“One of them.”

Leo blew across the cup. For a while they watched people move through the market, their coats touching occasionally at the shoulder because the bench was narrow.

Then Leo's phone rang. He looked at the screen and grimaced.

“Oh, shit.”

“What?”

“My mom.”

“Why is that shit?”

“Because I told her I was packing.”

Taiga looked around at the market.

Leo pointed at him. “You are now legally involved in the lie.”

“I am absolutely not.”

Leo answered anyway.

“Hola, mami.”

Taiga looked at him.

It wasn't just the language. Leo's whole rhythm changed with it. He spoke faster, softer, words running into each other in a way Taiga had never heard from him before.

“Sí, mañana. Ya revisé. No, el vuelo no está cancelado. Sí, tengo el boleto.” Leo closed his eyes briefly. “Mami, ya te mandé el boleto.”

Leo sighed.

“Sí, ya sé que nevó. Créeme, estoy viendo la nieve ahora mismo.”

Taiga drank his coffee. Leo looked at him and raised one warning finger.
Taiga smiled into the cup.

“Sí, tengo la bufanda puesta.”

A longer pause. Leo looked down at it.

“Sí, esa.”

Another pause.

“No, no se va a ofender. ¿Y por qué yo iba a mentir con una bufanda?”

Leo glanced at Taiga while he said it, then turned slightly away.

“Sí, comí.”

His tone shifted into the patient, mildly resigned cadence of somebody answering a familiar question.
Taiga looked over.
Leo nudged his shoe with one foot and kept listening.

“Sí, mami. Ya sé.”

The voice on the other end grew louder for several seconds. Leo sighed.

“Sí, ya sé. Llego mañana por la tarde. Dile a todos que les mando saludos. No, no despiertes a nadie cuando llegue. Tengo llaves.”

Another pause.

His expression softened.

“Yo también te quiero.”

Then, after another beat, “Sí. Chao. Chao, mami. Puedes colgar.”

He waited.

“Mami.”

Finally the call ended.

Taiga laughed.

Leo leaned back against the bench. “This is my future.”

Taiga looked at him for another second.

“What?”

“You sound different.”

Leo glanced over. “In Spanish?”

“Yeah.”

“Well. That's usually what happens when you change languages.”

“Not that, idiot. You talk faster.”

Leo smiled. “My mother talks faster. It's survival.”

“I understood snow.”

“Excellent.”

“And scarf.”

“Two words. Practically fluent.”

Leo blinked.

“That sounded weirdly surprised.”

“I thought you were from here when I met you.”

“Seriously?”

“Yeah.”

Leo seemed disproportionately pleased by that. “Okay, I'm taking that as a compliment.”

By the time we came here, the hard part wasn't really English anymore. “I absolutely have an accent.”

“Barely.”

"You have just turned that into a challenge."

“Anyway. My mother has confirmed that I possess a ticket, food, keys and the scarf my sister gave me, so international travel may now proceed.”

"Yeah."

Leo stayed quiet.

“Maybe.”

“Congratulations.”

From there the conversation moved without either of them having to steer it. They argued about Leo's sister's terrible scarf color, whether Acropolis Park would be impossible under snow, whether Hiro would attempt to cook for everybody at Camp Buddy over break—the answer was obviously yes—and whether Yoichi had ever met Caleb. He had not. Leo declared this a future scientific experiment. Taiga threatened him.

The market lights came on brighter as evening settled, and by the time Taiga checked the time they had been sitting there nearly forty minutes.

---

They left Central Park at seven-thirty, by which point the snow had won. The paths were white, cars moved slower, and every roof had a thin layer collecting along the edges.

Leo stopped beneath one of the lamps and looked up. “Okay, I know this is stupid because snow is objectively inconvenient, but this is fucking great.”

Taiga shoved his hands into his coat pockets. “You're going to say that until your flight gets cancelled.”

“If that happens, I'm blaming you.”

“Why?”

“You manifested negativity.”

“That isn't how weather works.”

“Prove it.”

Taiga kept walking, and Leo caught up.

The train back was busier, so they stood near the doors while the carriage swayed through the university district. Leo argued that weather prediction was mostly “educated pessimism,” and Taiga tried to explain why that sentence was offensive to statistics. Leo refused to concede anything.

Campus was almost empty when they stepped out. Snow softened the stone paths, the lawn and the roofs of the residence halls; even the tower looked less severe under white.

They walked toward the Residential Zone while Leo kicked lightly at a ridge of snow beside the path.

“So you're gone tomorrow.”

“Yeah.”

“Three weeks?”

“Yeah.”

Leo nodded. “I'll probably be back before New Year for a couple things.”

“Of course you have things.”

“I told you. Flexible.”

“That word still doesn't mean that.”

“I reject linguistic gatekeeping.”

“I'm glad you came,” Leo said.

Taiga looked at him. “Yeah.”

Leo grinned. “Also, for someone who had never skated, you only almost killed one civilian. That's genuinely impressive.”

“Go fuck yourself.”

“Why?”

“So I know you didn't get eaten by the angry dog.”

“Yuki likes me.”

“You've never met her.”

“I'm protecting myself from grief.”

“Oh! Taiga.”

“What?”

“Do not tell anyone I fell.”

“You didn't fall.”

“I almost fell.”

“You want me to hide an event that did not happen?”

“Yes.”

“Fine.”

Leo narrowed his eyes. “That sounded too easy.”

“Good night, Leo.”

“Taiga.”

“What?”

“If Enzo somehow knows by January, I'm blaming you.”

“Good night.”

Leo laughed. “Night!”

He walked away. Taiga watched until he reached the next path, then turned toward Residence.
