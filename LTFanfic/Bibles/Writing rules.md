# 2. Core Tone

The story should feel:
- grounded;
- lived-in;
- ordinary enough to make important moments matter;
- emotionally specific without constant melodrama.

The reader should feel university/camp life around the plot:
- walking;
- eating;
- changing clothes;
- carrying things;
- checking schedules;
- waiting;
- working;
- commuting;
- studying;
- cleaning;
- getting tired;
- wasting time;
- having conversations that are not about the central conflict.

Avoid:
- destiny language;
- constant foreshadowing;
- every scene delivering a lesson;
- characters orbiting Taiga because the plot needs them;
- romance forcing encounters;
- artificial cliffhangers;
- constant emotional analysis;
- every chapter needing a dramatic climax.

---

# 3. The Most Important New Craft Rule
## Write circumstances forward, not scenes backward from the plot beat

We may know out of story that:
- Taiga and Kieran will grow closer for a few days;
- Leo will become jealous;
- Kieran will reveal the old relationship;
- Kieran will leave Friday;
- Taiga and Leo will have an intimate Saturday night.

The characters do **not** know this outline.

Do not make their behavior visibly serve it.

Bad construction:
- Kieran wakes early with a second coffee prepared specifically because the chapter needs a bonding scene.
- Yoshinori announces on Monday that Kieran is staying until Friday because the outline says Friday is his departure.
- a work assignment exists only to trap Taiga and Kieran together for exposition.
- Leo walks into a scene exactly when he needs to witness jealousy evidence.

Better construction:
- Kieran is already drinking his own coffee; Taiga independently comes inside, gets a normal camp mug, and happens to sit nearby.
- Kieran stays without a fixed dramatic departure date; Friday becomes a consequential decision after Thursday.
- ordinary camp work sometimes places Taiga and Kieran together, sometimes does not.
- Leo and Yoichi naturally finish a task together and build their own friendship.
- old familiarity surfaces because something in the present reminds Kieran of the past.

Core principle:

> **Decide the destination as writers. Let the characters stumble into the route naturally.**

# 4. Not Every Scene Must Advance the Relationship

Mere coexistence is useful.

Taiga and Kieran can:
- work in the same space without a revelation;
- eat at the same table without old history emerging;
- pass tools;
- disagree about something trivial;
- go hours without speaking.

Taiga and Leo can:
- sit together without romantic tension;
- shower without a charged beat;
- work separately;
- have ordinary jokes;
- be tired.

A relationship feels real partly because it survives scenes that do not "progress" it.

If every interaction produces measurable romantic advancement, the outline becomes visible.

---

# 5. Causality Over Scheduling

Future consequences should not be pre-announced merely because the writers know them.

Examples:
- Kieran's Friday departure is not a booking on Yoshinori's calendar; it is his later choice.
- Leo's Thursday anger should grow out of what he learns and what has accumulated, not because Thursday is designated "fight day."
- Saturday intimacy should happen because Friday/Saturday repair reaches the threshold, not because Saturday is reserved for sex.

Plans are guide rails.

They are not dialogue instructions.

---

# 6. Realistic Physical Logistics Matter

Small logistical errors make grounded scenes feel artificial.

Check:
- what objects actually exist in the location;
- whether a character could reasonably know someone would arrive;
- whether people would use mugs, plates, towels, tools, phones, bags, etc. appropriate to the setting;
- travel distance and timing;
- who has keys/access;
- whether a task actually requires the people assigned to it.

Example:
Camp Buddy has a kitchen and crockery.

Default to a mug/cup, not a disposable paper cup, unless there is a reason paper cups are present.

---

# 7. Dialogue — Global Rule

Characters must sound like different people talking.

Do not turn dialogue into one author's efficient clipped style.

Conversations should contain:
- full sentences;
- explanations;
- interruptions;
- false starts;
- corrections;
- filler;
- overexplaining;
- awkwardness;
- misunderstandings;
- occasional rambling;
- unfinished thoughts.

Real people are not maximally efficient.

---

# 8. One-Word Dialogue

One-word replies are allowed but should be rare.

Drafting target:
**roughly 1% of dialogue turns**.

Prefer:

> "There's a problem on the trail."

over:

> "Trail."

Prefer:

> "Let me guess, a tree fell across it?"

over:

> "Tree?"

A single-word line earns its place when:
- brevity itself is funny;
- it is a Leo joke;
- Taiga is intentionally abrupt;
- a name carries emotional weight;
- shock cuts language short;
- the character genuinely would not say more.

Do not mechanically eliminate every one-word line.

Do eliminate one-word ping-pong as the default rhythm.

---

# 9. Never Comment on the Writing Rule Inside the Prose

Do not write meta-narration such as:

> One word. Allowed.

or:

> That was better.

because we had a style rule in mind.

The reader should not see the rubric.

If the line works, move on.

Writing rules are for drafting and revision, not for Taiga's narration to grade.

---

# 10. Repeated Dialogue Templates

Sharply reduce recurring exchanges such as:

> "Don't."
>
> "I didn't say anything."
>
> "You were about to."

Also reduce:
- "Don't start."
- "Don't say it."
- "Don't do that."
- Taiga seeing an amused face and preemptively shutting down a joke before anyone speaks.
- "You made the face."
- "I have a face."
- other reusable comedy templates.

These are not forbidden.

They become a problem when the reader recognizes the authorial template before the character.

Let people:
- actually say the joke;
- change the subject;
- ignore the expression;
- respond specifically to what was said;
- occasionally let a face pass without narration.

### Caleb / Taiga response motif

One recurring exchange is intentionally reserved for Caleb and Taiga.

Early version:
- Caleb gives a minimal answer;
- Taiga waits for more;
- Taiga asks some version of "That's it?";
- Caleb genuinely does not understand what else is expected.

As their friendship develops, Caleb should learn the pattern and pre-empt it:
- "You want me to say something else."
- "You want me to ask something else?"

Taiga's default answer can remain **no** for a long time.

A later **yes** should be treated as an emotional payoff: Taiga admitting that he actually wants reassurance, elaboration or company without being forced to formulate the entire request first.

Do not casually give this same exchange structure to Leo, Keitaro, Kieran or other characters. If they underreact, evade or challenge Taiga, let them do it in their own voice.

Yuri may say:
- "Kyaa~"

But not every line should be a ship joke.

Yoichi may use:
- "tch";
- "psh";
- "lame";
- profanity;
- nicknames.

But not every line needs all of them.

Kieran may stammer when exposed.

Do not make him stammer in every sentence.

The character should be recognizable from:
- rhythm;
- vocabulary;
- emotional behavior;
- social role;
- sentence construction;
- what they choose to notice.

Not one verbal badge.

# 14. Taiga Internal Narration

Avoid repetitive narration patterns such as:

> Taiga noticed Leo.
>
> Not because it mattered.
>
> Useless information.
>
> His brain filed it away anyway.

That pattern worked early when Taiga was rationalizing attention.

It is now overused and developmentally outdated.

By the current story:
- Taiga knows Leo matters;
- he knows he pays attention;
- he knows he is attracted.

Trust the reader.

Let noticing simply occur.

Also reduce:
- constant "why did I notice that?";
- constant self-scolding after every glance;
- over-explaining every blush.

Some reactions can remain uninterpreted.

# 28. Touch and Physical Intimacy

## Casual touch should usually remain casual

Do **not** make every Taiga/Leo touch become:

1. practical contact;
2. both freeze;
3. contact remains;
4. eye contact;
5. nobody moves;
6. narration announces "static."

Most touch should flow through the action.

Examples:
- wiping dirt from Leo's cheek;
- catching someone who stumbles;
- handing over tools;
- touching an arm in training;
- shoulder bumps;
- sitting close;
- steadying someone.

Attraction can register afterward:
- Leo blushes;
- Taiga notices what he did after his hand is already gone;
- one thinks about it later;
- neither creates unnecessary extra distance.

Reserve:
- freezing;
- deliberate lingering;
- mutual staring;
- "neither moved";

for moments where the physical boundary is genuinely changing.

A cute touch does not automatically need to become a scene.

# 30. Sexual Milestones

Sex can precede formal romance.

No kissing-before-romance rule remains a useful preference:
- deliberate sexual exploration can happen before a formal relationship;
- kissing can carry a more explicitly romantic threshold and may be saved for later.

Any deliberate sexual milestone must be:
- mutual;
- clearly consensual;
- emotionally understood;
- stoppable without punishment;
- not used to patch over an unresolved fight.

The emotional threshold matters more than the specific act.

# 31. Pacing

The story is slow burn, but "slow" does not mean "repeat the same uncertainty forever."

Progression should change the kind of uncertainty.

Examples:

Early:
> Why do I notice him?

Later:
> Why did that touch affect me?

Now:
> Did he mean to?

Next:
> Why am I jealous?

Later:
> What do I want to do about it?

Do not reset characters to earlier ignorance after they have learned something.

# 34. Scene Construction

Before writing a scene, ask:

1. Why are these characters in the same place?
2. What were they doing before the emotional beat appeared?
3. Would the scene still make sense if the emotional beat did not happen?
4. Does the setting create the opportunity naturally?
5. Is someone behaving with knowledge they do not possess?
6. Is an object/prop present for a believable reason?
7. Is the scene doing too many jobs at once?

If the only answer to #1 is "because we need them to bond/fight/flirt," rebuild the circumstance.

---

# 35. Let Consequences Produce Decisions

Important choices should often come **after** pressure accumulates.

Do not announce them early.

Examples:
- Kieran leaves Friday because Thursday changes what staying means.
- Leo distances himself because suppressed anger becomes hard to contain.
- Taiga pursues the issue because Leo's changed behavior becomes impossible to ignore.

This makes plot feel consequential rather than scheduled.

# 36. Relationship Conflict

Do not resolve conflict by declaring one character morally correct and the other stupid.

# 40. Revision Red Flags

When revising a chapter, search for:

## Narration
- telegraph prose built from repeated one-clause or fragment paragraphs;
- action chains such as "Leo did this. Taiga looked. He moved. They continued.";
- corrective fragments stacked for rhythm: "Not this. Not that. Something else.";
- noun-list beats used where a normal sentence would read more naturally;
- paragraph breaks that exist only to manufacture emphasis;
- long stretches where every narrative sentence has the same short subject-verb shape.

Short sentences and fragments are still available for genuine emphasis, panic, impact, or comic timing. They should be the exception inside otherwise discursive prose rather than the narrator's default cadence.

## Dialogue
- too many one-word replies;
- repeated "what / nothing";
- "don't / I didn't say anything / you were about to";
- every joke being a perfect comeback;
- everyone sounding clipped;
- catchphrase spam.

## Romance
- every touch becoming a freeze;
- every scene escalating;
- every Leo appearance becoming about attraction;
- Taiga narrating the same realization again;
- jealousy becoming ownership.

## Plot machinery
- characters appearing exactly when needed;
- people anticipating future events unrealistically;
- pre-scheduled emotional decisions;
- convenient props;
- assignments designed only to trap two characters together;
- scenes that announce their purpose.

## Character drift
- Taiga emotionally fluent too easily;
- Leo too terse;
- Yoichi becoming Taiga;
- Aiden becoming "Oho guy";
- Yuri becoming only a shipper;
- Kieran becoming a villain;
- Yoshinori becoming a therapist.

---

# 41. Final Writing Principle

The story should feel like **life happening under pressure**, not plot machinery disguised as life.

The outline belongs behind the curtain.

Characters should only know:
- what has happened;
- what they have been told;
- what they can reasonably infer;
- what they want right now.

Let:
- chores create proximity;
- schedules create absence;
- old habits surface by accident;
- feelings become visible through behavior;
- consequences create the next decision.

Most importantly:

> **Do not force a planned beat to happen. Create a believable situation in which it can happen, then let the characters earn it.**
